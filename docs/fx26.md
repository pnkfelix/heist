# FX-26

Direction set 2026-09-24. All seven steps of the plan are implemented, in
`crates/fixpt-fx26` and `fixpt --dialect fx26` (2026-09-25).

`docs/fx26-grammar.md` is a reference grammar of the forms, as the
parsers accept them.

FX-87 and FX-91 each took the effect-system research of their day and made a
language of it: Lucassen's effect *checking*, with regions and explicit
polymorphism, then Jouvelot and Talpin's *inference*, first-class modules, and
dependent types for them. Papers built on FX kept extending it:
- control effects for first-class continuations (Jouvelot & Gifford, PLDI '89);
- communication effects for parallel processes;
- static estimates of execution cost (Reistad & Gifford, LFP '94).

FX-26 carries that on here, with three decisions:

1. **It is the tooling's own language.** FX-26 exists so that fixpt's own
   machinery can be written in it: the eager reader, then the expander and
   the speculative checker (`TODO.md` §11). Features are chosen by what that
   machinery needs, not by a wish list.
2. **Effects are licences.** An effect FX-26 checks is there because
   something acts on it. The REPL runs code speculatively only when its effect
   allows it. The compiler removes or reorders code on the strength of a
   `Checked` fact carried through the metadata channel (`docs/macros.md`,
   `crates/fixpt-core/src/facts.rs`). Later, parallelism.
3. **Checking is bidirectional.** Definitions carry signatures, as in FX-87,
   and bodies are checked against them while types are inferred locally. Two
   reasons. Errors stay local, which is what checking as you type needs. And
   the checking direction always knows what type is *expected* at a point,
   which is the question `,help` asks of a hole.

## The kernel (reviewed 2026-09-25)

FX-26 declares a minimal kernel, following **KFX**, "the kernel language of
FX-87", over which PLDI '89 states its rules (PDF p. 2–3, proceedings
p. 219–220). The rest of FX-26 is defined by translation into it. KFX's
grammar, as the paper gives it:

```
K ::= region | effect | type
R ::= I | @I                                   region variable, region constant
F ::= I | pure | (write R) | (read R) | (alloc R) | (maxeff F0 F1)
T ::= I | (subr F (T) T) | (poly (I K) T) | (ref T R)
E ::= I | (lambda (I T) E) | (E0 E1) | (plambda (I K) E) | (proj E D)
      | (new R T E) | (get E) | (set E0 E1)
D ::= R | F | T
```

The paper assumes "the anti-aliasing rule of FX-87 is eliminated" (p. 4,
footnote 2). So do we, until something needs it.

Masking applies at every subexpression that combines effects, as in FX-87's
reference (`erase-effect` in `desc-of-begin`, `desc-of-lambda`,
`desc-of-letrec` and `desc-of-app`), extended with the control-effect
conditions of p. 6.

Base types (`int`, `bool`, `char`, `string`, `unit`) are identifiers bound in
the initial environment, as they are in FX-87, not grammar.

**Programs** sit above the kernel. A program is a sequence of top-level
forms, and each may add to the environment that the forms after it see
(`crates/fixpt-fx26/src/top.rs`):

```
P ::= (define I T E)         a definition of type T; if E is a lambda, I is in scope in it
    | (define I E)           a definition, with E's own type
    | (define-rec (I T L) …) procedures that call each other; each L a lambda
    | (define-type I T)      a type abbreviation; T may mention I
    | E
```

**A definition sees only the definitions before it** (since 2026-09-26).
Nothing is declared ahead, so recursion's knot is never tied where a
program could see it half done:

- A definition that is a lambda is in scope in itself. Making a lambda
  runs nothing, so nothing can call it before it exists.
- Procedures that call each other are one `define-rec`, a top-level
  `letrec`.
- A `letrec`, like a `define-rec`, binds only lambdas.

A knot of anything else is written with an I-cell or a `ref`, and the
types say so. Types are still declared ahead, so they may refer to each
other in any order: each `define-type` (and so each `define-datatype`)
defined once by name is in scope before any is read, and a cycle among
them must pass through a constructor (2026-09-30). A name a program's
`define-type` defines twice is not declared ahead: each type sees the
definition before it, and every value the last, and `fixpt` warns of it
(a line of `check`'s; on `run`'s and `eval`'s standard error). A module
refuses it. The options considered are in
`docs/research/recursion-and-initialization.md`.

At the REPL, where entries come one at a time, an entry defining types
that names types not defined yet is held, pending, rather than refused,
and runs with the entry that defines the last of them, all together as a
file's types are (`Fx26Session::consider`). Only types make an entry
wait: what is missing is found by standing `int` in for each missing
name until the group checks, and anything else wrong is reported as
ever. While typing, the line under the form says whether entering it
would wait, or complete what waits; `,pending` lists what waits, and
`,pending clear` drops it.

A local `letrec`'s procedures need not name the globals they read
(2026-09-30): where the group does not check at the types written, each
is checked as though its type said it read any global, the globals its
body reads are put in its type, as `define*` does for a definition, and
again until the group's calls of each other add none; then the group is
checked at those types. An effect mismatch prints both whole types, and
then, on a line of its own, what the one found has beyond what is
expected.

`define-type` exists because recursive types are common here: a continuation
that takes a pair of continuations, as in C7, is unreadable written out at
every use. A message shows a type a `define-type` named by its name. Naming
is by identity, and substitution copies what it walks, so a type that is
data and mentions no variable (base types, unions, sums, products, pairs,
references and arrays, at regions that are constants: a `define-datatype`,
`datum`) is noted when its definition is done, declared ahead or not, and
substitution leaves it itself (2026-10-08; `closed_named`,
`k-closed-named`): `(cons (leaf 2) l)` is a `(pairof tree (listof tree
@heap) r)`. An instance of a family, `(stack int)`, is still shown
expanded: it is expanded where it is used (`TODO.md` §60).

### Redefinition (2026-09-28)

A global's uses always refer to what it is now, in files and at the REPL
alike, as in Larceny. A second `define` of a name:

- **at a type every use can take** (a subtype of the old: usually the same
  type) assigns the global. Code defined before it sees the new value:

  ```
  (define f (subr pure (int) int) (lambda (n) (+ n 1)))
  (define g (subr pure (int) int) (lambda (n) (f n)))
  (define f (subr pure (int) int) (lambda (n) (* n 10)))
  (g 1)                                  ; 10: g calls the f there is now
  ```

- **at any other type** makes a new global, and every earlier definition
  that uses the name (its expressions' free variables), and every one that
  uses those, is checked again, in order. Each that still checks is
  defined again right there, by the same rule. Each that does not is
  *broken*: a later use of it is an error that says why, until it is
  defined again:

  ```
  (define f (subr pure (string) int) (lambda (s) (string-length s)))
  (g 1)   ; `g` is broken, since `f` was redefined (argument 1 is a int,
          ; where a string is expected): define it again to use it
  ```

  Code that holds an old procedure (in a data structure, say) keeps
  running it with the old global, which keeps the old type: nothing ever
  reads a value at a type it was not checked at.

**To keep a value as it was, bind it** when the definition runs:

```
(define d (let ((g g)) (lambda ((x int)) (g x))))   ; the g there is now
```

**At the REPL, re-runs wait** (the user's, 2026-10-05; `TODO.md` §29).
A load of a file whose later forms use earlier ones would otherwise run
each definition again for every redefinition before it, though the load
is about to define it again: quadratic. So at the REPL a redefinition at
another type leaves the definitions that use the name *out of date*,
neither checked nor run again. Each keeps the old global, as code holding
an old procedure does, so nothing reads a value at a type it was not
checked at, and nothing is broken. Only those that use the name itself
are out of date: one that uses an out-of-date definition sees it as it
is. Defining one again brings it up to date. The REPL says when what is
out of date changes; `,list-outdated` names them and what each uses that
was defined again; `,rerun-outdated` defines each again, oldest first, by
the rule above, until none is left that checks (one that does not stays
out of date, usable, saying why). Both checkers take this as a setting
(`Checker::defer_reruns`, `check-defer-reruns!`); `fixpt eval` and
`fixpt check` run files by the rule above.

Where re-runs do not wait, a redefinition that would break definitions is
asked about at a terminal, breaking them by default; `,redefine r`
refuses the next one instead, changing nothing. At the REPL, where
re-runs wait, neither is ever reached; both are to be removed (`TODO.md`
§32). The two checkers
(`Checker::top_defining`, `check.fx`'s `k-defining`) say what a form
runs; the lowering, the compilers and the evaluator run just that
(`checked-tops`), assigning a global or making a new one as it says.

A procedure's calls of itself by its own name are uses of its global
too, as in Larceny: after a redefinition, even a closure of the old
procedure still held somewhere calls the new one. A procedure that must
call itself binds itself locally, and its calls are then direct:

```
(define fib (subr spin (int) int)
  (letrec ((fib (subr spin (int) int) (lambda (n) (if (< n 2) n (+ (fib (- n 1)) (fib (- n 2)))))))
    fib))
```

A procedure that may reach itself through a global says `spin` (the
user's, 2026-09-28): defining `f` writes the global `f`, and a procedure
stored there whose calls read `f` (see "Globals as a region", below) may
reach itself, however it does: calling itself by name, calling something
that calls it, or calling a procedure kept as it was, `(let ((h h)) …)`,
that calls it (`programs/redefine/knot-kept.fx`). The termination check
trusts every procedure of a type without `spin` to end, and a cycle
through the store is one it never saw; so it is the rule for a procedure
kept in a ref, for globals. A recursive procedure proved to end, and
`pure`, calls itself through a local `letrec`, as `fib` above does.

**Globals as a region** (2026-09-28, with the user; both checkers). A
global's binding is a region, read by naming it
and written by defining it: naming `g` has the effect `(read (globals
g))`. `(globals f g)` in an effect is each of them, `(read (globals f
g))` being `(maxeff (read (globals f)) (read (globals g)))`, and
`@globals` is every global, past and future, so each `(read (globals g))`
is within `(read @globals)`. Globals are a region only in effects, only
read and written, and never masked. A procedure's type then says which
globals calling it reads, as the programmer chooses: precisely, as
`@globals`, or through an effect variable, for a procedure that calls
one it is given:

```
(define limit int 10)
(define below (subr (read (globals limit)) (int) bool) (lambda (x) (< x limit)))
(define clamp (subr (read @globals) (int) int) (lambda (x) (if (below x) x limit)))
```

`(define* name type lambda)` finds the globals instead, precisely: its
type says the rest, and the globals its body reads are added to its
latent effect, those the procedures it calls read included, and its own
name if it calls itself. It is checked twice: first as though its type
read `@globals`, which finds what it reads; then again at the type it
gets, so that what is kept is a check at that type:

```
(define* clamp (subr pure (int) int) (lambda (x) (if (below x) x limit)))
;; clamp : (subr (read (globals below limit)) (int) int)
```

So a recursive top-level procedure says it reads itself, or is a
`define*`; a `define-rec` group's members each read the whole group, as
`(read (globals ev od))`; and a prompt tag says the globals its bodies
read. Reading globals is licensed (`licence.rs`): no one else sees it.
The front end written in FX-26 says `(read @globals)`, through its effect
abbreviations (`checks`, `compiles`, `parses`, …) and in the types of
procedures written out: it never redefines its own, so precision would
buy it nothing.

A redefinition is compatible only if what it reads is within what the old
one read, since that is part of its type. A procedure stored in global
`f` whose latent effect reads `(globals f)`, or, for a `define-rec`, any
of its group, says `spin` (`no_reaching_itself`, `k-no-reaching-itself`).
One that reads `@globals` may read `f` only once `f` is a global: at its
first definition nothing defined before it refers to it, and what later
does is a write checked in its turn; so the front end's `(read @globals)`
needs no `spin`.

**Recursive types, named or not** (2026-09-27;
`docs/research/recursive-subtyping.md`). Recursive types are equi-recursive:
a type is a graph, a cycle is a recursive type, and subtyping compares
graphs, assuming a pair already being compared (Amadio and Cardelli). A
`define-type`'s name is transparent, a label on its cycle, and `(mu t T)`
writes one with no name, the same as `(dletrec ((t T)) t)`; so a named type
and its `mu` are each a subtype of the other. A recursive type with no name
prints as `(mu %d …)`, `%d` the cycle's depth, and reads back. A cycle must
pass through a constructor (a `poly` is not one, nor a generative type
whose representation is one of what it is given, since it is that:
`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`, A2). Frozen pairs, at `(const
p)`, are covariant, as frozen bloblets are.

**`spin`: code that may run for an unbounded time** (2026-09-27;
`docs/research/type-and-effect-directions.md`, R6). An effect atom with no
region, so nothing masks it. A call has it when it is a call of its own
recursive group (from inside the group's lambdas), or a call of a
procedure not known, whose type is recursive through a parameter
(self-application); otherwise a call has what the callee's latent effect
says. A recursive definition's type must say `spin`, unless every run of
its group ends. Naming a member of such a group anywhere but as a call's
operator says `spin` too: it gives the member away, and whoever calls it
may loop through it. A procedure kept in
mutable storage at a region, whose latent effect reads that region, must
say `spin` too: that is how a knot through the store shows, since such a
procedure could fetch itself. A call of `cwcc` says `spin` unless its
receiver is a `lambda` whose continuation is named only as the operator of
calls in its body, outside any `lambda` or prompt: only then can the
continuation be called only while `cwcc` runs, to leave it. Called after
`cwcc` has returned, it comes back to it, as often as it is called:
recursion with no procedure at all (`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`,
F3). Which procedures are known is by binding, not by name and type, so a
parameter that shadows one is not taken for it (F1).

**Well-founded recursion** (2026-09-27; `src/terminate.rs`, and
`k-terminates?` in `check.fx`). A recursive group needs no `spin` when
size-change termination (Lee, Jones and Ben-Amram, POPL 2001) shows every
run ends. Each call within the group is a graph of how the callee's
arguments relate to the caller's parameters. The graphs are closed under
composition, and every graph from a member to itself that is its own
composition must have a parameter strictly smaller. The measures:
- **parts**: a `tagcase` arm's value or fields, an `extract`, the `car`
  or `cdr` of a pair at an `acyclic` region (or of a union whose pair
  member is one, as `datum`'s: a `car` that returns took that member
  apart).
  Each was made before what holds it, and cannot change;
- **down**: an integer less by a literal, under a test bounding the
  parameter below by something fixed for the whole recursion: a literal, a
  variable bound outside the group, a parameter every call in the group
  passes on unchanged, or `string-length`, `+` or `-` of such (so `(< i
  (string-length s))` bounds `i` when `s` is passed on);
- **up**: greater by a literal, under a bound above.

So `(define len (subr pure ((listof int acyclic) int) int) …)` over `(cdr
xs)`, a countdown under `(> n 0)`, a tree walk by `tagcase`, `even?` and
`odd?` together, and Ackermann's function all check as `pure`. Not
recognised: a list at a region that may be written (it may be cyclic);
`(= n 0)` as the only test (`(f -1)` loops); and a part reached through
another procedure's parameter, as in a rose tree walked by an inner loop
(`tests/programs/terminate/rose-tree.fx`).

A primitive that walks a list (`reverse`, `append`, `list->string`,
`datum-list`, …) ends on a cyclic one, with an error: its type says no
`spin`. A typed way from a list that may be cyclic to a finite one, with a
continuation for a cycle, is `confirm`'s job
(`docs/research/confirmation.md`).

Finite data needs no `letfreeze` when it is built in order: `cons` may
allocate straight into `acyclic`, since frozen data cannot be written, and
a pair whose tail is a `(listof T acyclic)` is one. The parser's trees are
built so, `(listof exp acyclic)` and the rest, with `@a` left for nothing
the parser writes; so the compiler's and checker's walks of them end.

**Effect summaries, for a compiler** (2026-09-28). Each checker notes,
for every expression it checks (in either direction), a summary of its
effect, each a stronger claim than the one before: 0 pure (no atom at all,
so no `spin` either), 1 reads only, 2 anything else, 3 anything else that
may also keep its continuation for later (`comefrom`), write a global, or
do what an effect variable stands for (any of which a global's value may
change across, within one run of the code); by where the expression
starts and ends; where two share a
span (a form the reader expands into several), the greater. The Rust
checker's `effect_summaries`, and `check.fx`'s `checked-effects`; a test
(`checker.rs`, `effect_summaries_agree`) requires them to agree on every
test program. They are what a transformation asks before it moves,
reuses, evaluates or drops an expression; no compiler uses them yet.

**Regions and places** (2026-09-27; `docs/research/places-and-regions.md`).
A *region* is for analysis: a name in types and effects, erased before
anything runs. A *place* is for allocation: memory that data is put in.
Plain `cons`, `new` and the rest allocate in the collected heap, whatever
region their types name.

**Regions that end: `letregion`, `letrena` and `letreap`** (2026-09-26).
`(letregion r body …)`, `(letrena r body …)` and `(letreap r body …)` bind
a region variable `r` for the body's types. The body's value may not
mention `r`, latent effects included, and no continuation captured in the
body may outlive it (no `comefrom` in its masked effect). What the body
does to `r` is masked, so a procedure that builds and uses its own data in
a region is `pure`. The three forms have the same typing rule and differ
only in the place they make for the region:
- `letregion` makes none: its data is the heap's;
- `letrena` makes an arena, reclaimed only when the body ends;
- `letreap` makes a heap of its own, which the collector may also collect
  while the body runs.

`letrena` and `letreap` bind `r` as a *place*, a kind of its own: a place
is also a region (the region of the data allocated in it directly), so a
place may stand wherever a region may, but not the other way round. A
`poly` may bind one, `(p place)`. The name `r` is also a variable, of type
`(place r)`: the place as a value. An allocation goes to a place only when
it is given one:

| operation                     | type                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `(rcons r x y)`               | `(poly ((r place)) (poly ((t1 type) (t2 type)) (subr (alloc r) ((place r) t1 t2) (pairof t1 t2 r))))` |
| `(rnew r v)`                  | `(poly ((r place)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (alloc r) ((place r) t) (ref t r))))`                       |
| `(rmake-array r n v)`         | `(poly ((r place)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (alloc r) ((place r) int t) (arrayof t r))))`               |
| `(rmake-icell r)`             | `(poly ((r place)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (alloc r) ((place r)) (icell t r))))`                       |
| `(rmake-bloblet r bytes e …)` | as `make-bloblet`, in `r`'s place                                                                     |

`(rlambda r (param …) body …)` is a `lambda` whose closure is made in
`r`'s place. Calling it reads the closure, so its latent effect has
`(read r)`, and so its type mentions the region and it cannot leave the
body; making it has the effect `(alloc r)`. A `plambda` whose body is an
`rlambda` may be generalized though that is an effect, since making a
closure makes no mutable data a type could be generalized over; so there
is no separate `rplambda`.

Plain `cons` allocates in the heap, whatever region its type names, which
is always sound. A procedure made in the body that allocates in `r`
mentions `r`, and so captures it like any other variable; a place value,
like anything whose type mentions the region, cannot leave the body.

Each place is chunks of memory of its own, all given back when the body
ends, however it ends (`crates/fixpt-heap/src/heap/regions.rs`); so the
body is not in tail position. A `letrena`'s chunks the collector treats as
roots and never moves; a `letreap`'s it collects with the heap, copying
what is reachable in it into new chunks of its own, so a reap given much
more than it keeps stays small. The evaluator written in FX-26 erases
regions and places: everything it allocates is the heap's.

**Regions in a place, and the order of lifetimes** (2026-09-27). The
allocators take a place and a region: `rcons`'s type is `(poly ((p place)
(r region p)) (poly ((t1 type) (t2 type)) (subr (maxeff (alloc r) (alloc
p)) ((place p) t1 t2) (pairof t1 t2 r))))`. The binder `(r region p)` is a
region that won't outlive `p`: an instantiation must give a region that
won't, and a region nothing else fixes defaults to `p` itself, so `(rcons p
x y)` allocates at `p`'s own region, as it always did. `(alloc p)` keeps
the place live in the effect of whatever allocates into it. "Won't
outlive" is decided by nesting: a region or place bound inside another
(by `letregion`, `letrena`, `letreap`, `letfreeze`, or as a `plambda`'s
binder around its body) won't outlive it; `@` constants and `const` never
end. So one arena may hold data at several regions bound inside it, and a
procedure may take `(p place)` and `(r region p)` from its caller.

**Freezing: `letfreeze` and `const`** (2026-09-27;
`docs/research/places-and-regions.md`). `(letfreeze r body …)` binds a
region `r` as `letregion` does, and its value leaves with `r` replaced by
`const`, the region of frozen data:
- nothing may write `const`: `set-car!` on a `(listof int const)` is refused,
  wherever it happens, since what is done to `const` is never masked;
- reading `const`, and making data at it, are pure;
- the value may not keep a way to write `r` (a closure whose latent effect
  writes it is refused).

It is sound because nothing mentioning `r` leaves the body but its value,
so when it ends the value is the only way into `r`'s data. It costs nothing
at run time: nothing is copied or marked.

**Finite data.** A cycle in data needs a write after the data is made (a
`set-cdr!`, a `set` of a reference, an array's or an I-cell's), and every
such write is `(write r)`. So a `letfreeze` whose body never writes its
region, only builds it, gives `acyclic` (or `(acyclic p)`) rather than
`const`: frozen data no cycle runs through, all of it, lists and trees
alike. `(listof T acyclic)` is a finite list of unknown length. (The
region was spelled `finite` until 2026-09-28; `finite` is now only a
size, below, and the region is named for what it promises;
`docs/research/acyclic-regions.md` has the design, and its costs.) Finite data
is `const` data too, not the other way round. The writes are noted as
masking sees them, before it could hide them.

`(letfreeze (r p) body …)` freezes into place `p` instead of the heap: the
value's `r` becomes `(const p)`, frozen data that won't outlive `p`. Its
data must be in `p` or a place outliving it, so `r` is ordered below only
`p` and what outlives `p`, and an allocation into a place that ends first
is refused. `heap` names the collected heap as a place; `const` is `(const
heap)`. Reading data frozen into the heap is pure, since the heap never
ends; reading data frozen into a place is an effect on it, `(read (const
p))`, which ties a closure that reads it to `p`, and which masking removes
where `p` is no longer seen (`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`, F2).

**I-cells: recursion's knot, made explicit** (prototype, 2026-09-26).
Arvind's I-structures. An `(icell T R)` is empty until its one write of a
`T`, and never changes after it:

| operation          | type                                                                    |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `(make-icell)`     | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (alloc r) () (icell t r))))` |
| `(icell-put! c v)` | `… (subr (init r) ((icell t r) t) unit)`                                |
| `(icell-get c)`    | `… (subr (await r) ((icell t r)) t)`                                    |

`(await R)` is a new effect. A read waits for the write, so it must stay
after writes to `R`, but it commutes with other reads. It is masked like
`read`. A write is `(init R)` (2026-10-13), FX's: within `(write R)`, and
told apart only by `par`, against which it commutes with reads and
awaits ("Processes and `par`", below). `icell-get` and `icell-put!` are
forms since then, not procedures.

A read of an empty cell waits for a task to write it (a process, or a
branch of `par`: "Processes and `par`"). With none left that can, it is
an error, a deadlock; so is a second write. Mutual recursion ties its
knot with cells:

```
(define parity (subr pure (int) bool)
  (lambda (n)
    (let ((ev (the (icell (subr (await @p) (int) bool) @p) (make-icell)))
          (od (the (icell (subr (await @p) (int) bool) @p) (make-icell))))
      (begin
        (icell-put! ev (lambda ((k int)) (if (= k 0) #t ((icell-get od) (- k 1)))))
        (icell-put! od (lambda ((k int)) (if (= k 0) #f ((icell-get ev) (- k 1)))))
        ((icell-get ev) n)))))
```

Nothing outside `parity` names `@p`, so masking removes the cells'
effects: the knot is visible in the source, and `parity` is still `pure`.
`letrec`, `define-rec` and a lambda's `define` need no cells: they bind
only lambdas, so no knot of theirs can be seen half tied. I-cells are for
the knots that are not procedures, or that are tied across separately
loaded code. The other options, and why these were chosen, are in
`docs/research/recursion-and-initialization.md`.

## Control, typed

**From the paper (PLDI '89; `DONE.md` §11 summarises it).** Two control effects
on regions: `(goto r)`, for an expression that may not return to its
continuation, and `(comefrom r)`, for one that may keep its continuation for
later. `cwcc` is typed

```
(poly (r region) (poly (t type) (poly (e effect)
  (subr (maxeff (comefrom r) e)
        ((subr e ((subr (goto r) (t) void)) t))
        t))))
```

Both effects are masked when the expression neither imports variables nor
returns values whose types mention `r`. The paper shows why the `goto` rule has
to be stricter than FX-87's masking of memory effects. Its examples are the
first conformance cases, because no implementation survives: the archived
FX-87 has none.

**Ours to design: delimited control (settled in step 3, 2026-09-25).** The
paper types full continuations. The tooling uses prompts, composable
continuations and marks. The hypothesis this section used to state held, with
two changes that step 3 found necessary; `crates/fixpt-fx26/tests/control.rs`
has a case for each rule, and four deliberate mutations of the checker each
fail at least one of them.

- **A prompt tag lives in a region, and fixes what crosses its prompts:**
  `(prompt-tag A H D R)`. Its prompts deliver an `A`, its aborts carry an
  `H`, and — the first change — its delimited computations have effect at
  most `D`, apart from control on `R`. `D` is needed because a composable
  continuation *returns*: calling it runs the rest of a prompt body, and its
  type has to say what that does. The bound is checked at every prompt for
  the tag, as the answer type is. Fixing `A` rules out answer-type
  modification: `shift k k` is rejected, and so is any handler that returns
  something other than an `A`.
- **Capturing and aborting are control effects on the tag's region.**
  `call-with-composable-continuation` has `(comefrom R)`, and
  `abort-current-continuation` has `(goto R)` and returns `void`. A captured
  continuation has type `(composable T A D R)`, which is a subroutine
  `(subr (maxeff D (goto R) (comefrom R)) (T) A)` whose marks can also be read
  (`marks-of`).
- **A one-shot continuation** (2026-10-15, `TODO.md` §98, kayak's
  request): `call-with-oneshot-continuation`, typed as
  `call-with-composable-continuation` is, gives the procedure a
  `(oneshot T A D R)`, which may be resumed at most once; a second resume
  fails, "a one-shot continuation resumed twice", on every machine. That
  run-time failure is what defines the meaning, so an implementation that
  cannot resume a continuation twice (kayak's, where it is a Rust future)
  means the same. One-shot is in the type, so the capture says what the
  continuation will become: called as a composable one is, with the same
  effects, and a subroutine where one is wanted; but neither a
  `composable` nor a `oneshot` stands for the other, nor can its marks be
  read (`marks-of` takes a `composable`). At most once is not checked
  statically, which would need affine types. Machines keep a flag in the
  continuation they took (`Shots`, `fixpt_heap`), so nothing captured is
  copied for it. Tests: `control/oneshot-*.fx`, `run/oneshot-once.fx`,
  `run/oneshot-twice.fx` (`fixpt-cli/tests/one_shot.rs`, every machine).
- **The prompt delimits, and so it is syntax: `(prompt tag body handler)`** —
  the second change. It removes `(goto R)` and `(comefrom R)` from the body's
  effect. A constant could not do this: masking by region would see the tag
  itself, a free variable whose type mentions `R`, and keep everything. The
  condition is not the paper's either. A region holds many tags, and an abort
  to another of them passes straight through, so the prompt delimits only
  when the body can reach no tag in `R` except this one. That means no free
  variable of the body has a type mentioning `R`, apart from the tag itself
  when it is a variable. A tag the body makes for itself is harmless: aborting
  to it without a prompt of its own inside the body is an error, not a jump
  past this one.
- **An escape-only tag** (2026-10-12, `TODO.md` §89): `(escape-tag A H
  R)`, made by `make-escape-tag` and escaped to by `escape`, of `(goto
  R)`, as `abort-current-continuation` is to a prompt tag. Nothing is
  captured with one (`call-with-composable-continuation` takes a
  `prompt-tag`), so its prompts bound nothing their bodies do: it has no
  `D`. A prompt delimits for one as for a prompt tag. At run time it is a
  prompt tag; to the safety analyses a tag in `R` (generative type 9,
  `check::ESCAPE_TAG`). It is what raising and handling are built on:
  `(load-module "fx26:conditions.fx")`, a module built in, gives
  `handle`, `with-handler` and `raise` (`docs/research/conditions.md`,
  section 6b). Tests: `control/escape-tag*.fx`, `run/conditions.fx`.
- **A mark key is a location in the dynamic context.** `(mark-key T R)`
  names one. `with-mark` (a procedure taking a thunk, where Scheme's form is
  syntax) writes it; `first-mark`, `current-marks` and `marks-of` read it. So
  the region discipline for ordinary state applies, and a private key's
  marks are masked as a private cell's contents are.
- **Lists are FX-87's**, since marks come back as one, with one change:
  `(pairof A B R)` is a pair and never `nil` (2026-10-07, PLAN Q7's first
  stage, `docs/research/logical-types.md` L0). `(listof T R)` is `nil` or a
  pair whose tail is the list again; `(union nil (pairof A B R))` is "a
  pair, or none" (a table's entry, say). `cons` makes a `pairof`, which
  fits both; `car`, `cdr`, `set-car!`, `set-cdr!` take a pair that may be
  `nil`, and the compilers check it. `nil` is typed as a list, `(poly ((r
  region) (t type)) (listof t r))`, so `(proj nil @r int)` is the empty
  `(listof int @r)`; where a pair that may be `nil` is expected whose tail
  is no list, or a union, `nil` is of the type `nil`, which is below each.
  Where nothing says which list (2026-10-08, `TODO.md` §48), `nil` is of
  the type `nil` too: an argument whose parameter neither the expected
  result nor any other argument has solved (`(null? nil)`), heard after
  all of them; and an `if`'s branch, or a `tagcase`'s arm, beside others,
  is what they are if `nil` is one of them (`(if c nil xs)` is `xs`'s
  type; `(if c nil nil)`, `nil`), and beside a pair makes it one that may
  be `nil`. A new pair's tail solved to `nil` alone, nothing expected of
  the pair, is widened to a list of its head: `(cons 1 nil)` is a
  `(pairof int (listof int R) R)`. A pair can be written, so its tail is
  invariant and `nil` is not a list there by subtyping; the pair being new,
  no alias sees the narrower tail, so choosing the list is sound. An
  expected type decides instead: `(pairof int nil R)` keeps `nil`. A `let` of
  `nil` stays polymorphic, so each use of it is one of these. `(car nil)`
  is still an error: nothing says what its `car` would be.
  `no-pair` is the same value as "a pair, or none", `(poly ((r region) (t1
  type) (t2 type)) (union nil (pairof t1 t2 r)))`.
- **Unions of shapes** (2026-10-07, Q7's second stage, `logical-types.md`
  L1): `(union T …)`, of two members or more, is a value of any of them;
  their shapes at run time must differ, so a test can tell them apart: an
  integer (`int`, the fixed widths, `nat`), `f64`, `f32`, `char`, `bool`,
  `nil`, a pair, `string`, `symbol` (`unit`, `#u`, is one), a procedure,
  a bloblet (an array or `(bloblet …)`), a `ref`, a sum, a product, a
  `vector`, a `bytevector` (2026-10-08, as a reader makes them; opaque,
  `vector?` and `bytevector?` their tests, as `f64?` is `f64`'s). A type
  variable, an abstract or generative type, a tag or a key has no
  known shape and is no member (yet); `(union int t)` with `t` itself is
  refused. `false` (2026-10-07, Q7's stage 3) is the type of `#f` alone,
  below `bool` and of its shape, so `(union false int)`, "an int or none",
  is one, which `bool?` takes apart (`(union false bool)` is refused); `#f`
  is a `bool`, and checks as a `false` where one is expected, or a union
  with one. Unions are normal: one in another is flattened, `nil` beside a
  pair is the pair that may be `nil`. A member is below its union, a
  union below what each member is below; a recursive type may run through
  one, `(define-type lseq (union nil (pairof int (union lseq gen) @heap)))`.
- **What a test proves is in its type** (2026-10-07; Typed Racket's
  latent propositions): a procedure's result may be `(bool (then P …)
  (else Q …))`, a `bool` that proves each `P` of its arguments where it is
  true and each `Q` where false, each about arguments by their
  parameters' numbers from 0: `(shape i S)` or `(not (shape i S))`, `S` a
  shape's name (`int` … `bloblet`, `box`, `sum`, `product`), which narrows
  a variable; `(acyclic i)`, `(nat i)`, `(length i j)`, which certify one
  (for `certify-acyclic`, `certify-nat`, `certify-length`); `(< a b)`,
  `(<= a b)`, `(= a b)`, `(not (= a b))` of sizes, a term `i` an argument
  as a natural, `(length i)` its `nlist`'s length, `(lit k)` a natural,
  which give the size facts. The standard tests say theirs so: `<` is
  `(subr pure (int int) (bool (then (< 0 1)) (else (<= 1 0))))`, `null?`
  also `(= (length 0) (lit 0))` where true, `length-is?` `(length 0 1)`.
  The fixed widths' comparisons (`u32=`, `i64<` …) say what `int`'s do
  (2026-10-08: a fixed-width value is no size yet, so they give no fact
  today); `zero?` `(= 0 (lit 0))`, or its negation; `eq?` `(= 0 1)` where
  true only, since equal bignums may not be identical; the floats'
  comparisons nothing, since with a NaN `(not (< a b))` is not `(<= b a)`.
  A call's own type is `bool`; what it proves is of its arguments, there
  (2026-10-08: the checkers read every test's facts so, none by name).
- **A test narrows a path too** (2026-10-08): an argument that is a path
  from a variable, `(car x)`, `(cdr (car x))`, `(extract p l)`, is
  narrowed as a variable is, and the path, used again, has that type,
  while nothing may have changed what it reads: until the first
  expression, in the order things are evaluated, whose effect writes a
  region the path reads through (a pair's), or one that may be it (a
  region variable may be any region), or transfers control (`goto`,
  `comefrom`, `await`, an effect not known). Effects are per region, so
  aliasing is no matter: a write through any alias is a write to the
  region. Frozen data and products' fields are never written, so their
  paths' facts last the branch. A closure's body may run later: no
  path's fact holds in it (a variable's does; variables never change).
  `@heap` is an ordinary region here: where data is made by default, so a
  write to it ends the facts of every path through the heap's pairs, and
  data in a region of its own keeps them.
- **Concurrency, as the effects say** (the rule the above rests on,
  2026-10-08): only tasks whose effects do not interfere, none writing a
  region another reads or writes, may run interleaved but at their own
  suspension points, and every suspension point is a control effect (an
  abort to the scheduler's prompt is a `goto`, an I-cell's read an
  `await`). So an expression's effect is all that can change what it
  reads while it runs (Lucassen and Gifford's FX; `docs/research/async.md`,
  S8, preemption only for tasks that do not interfere). A test proving more is below one proving less, and
  any is below `bool`, so an alias of `int?` may promise less. A `lambda`
  is not yet checked against such a type (its body proves nothing), so
  only the standard predicates have one so far (`TODO.md` §54). `(bool
  …)` is a procedure's result only.
- **The shape predicates narrow** a variable, where they hold to its
  members of that shape and where not to the rest, as their types say:
  `int?`, `f64?`, `f32?`, `char?`, `bool?`, `null?`, `pair?`, `string?`,
  `symbol?`, `procedure?`, `array?`, `ref?` (shape `box`), `sum?`,
  `product?`, `vector?`, `bytevector?`, each of any value (2026-10-08:
  `f32?`, `ref?`, `sum?` and `product?`, whose shapes the checker had
  without a predicate, found missing by the evaluator's rewrite), `int?`'s `(poly ((t type))
  (subr pure (t) (bool (then (shape 0 int)) (else (not (shape 0
  int))))))`; `null?` and `pair?`
  also make a list, or "a pair, or none", a `pairof` where it is no `nil`.
  Through `not`, and through `and` and `or` as the size facts go (`(if a b
  #f)`, `(if a #t b)`). Variables are immutable, so the narrowing holds
  through the whole branch, closures in it included. `(typecase e (shape
  name body) … (else body))`, or `(else name body)`, is those tests in
  turn, each arm binding its member, the shapes `int`, `char`, `bool`,
  `nil`, `pair`, `string`, `symbol`, `procedure`, `bloblet`, `f64`, `f32`,
  `box`, `sum`, `product`, `vector`, `bytevector`; a variable
  `e` is tested as it is, so the `else` sees it narrowed. Natively each
  is in line (`DONE.md` §56): a tag, or a bloblet's header's kind.

One consequence for writing FX-26: a helper that aborts to a tag in `@r`, and
is bound with a type that mentions `@r`, blocks delimiting wherever it is
used, because it could abort to any tag in `@r`. Written region-polymorphically,
as the standard operations are, it does not.

**What "licence to speculate" means.** It cannot mean "pure". The eager reader
hands its checkpoint continuation back to its caller, so its `comefrom` on
the reader's region is never masked. It is safe because every effect it has
is on a region the caller owns: allocation, and control on its own prompt. The
licence the REPL checks is therefore: *no `read` or `write` on any region the
REPL shares with the user's program*. Control and allocation on regions the
driver owns are allowed.

## Bloblets (M12 step 6, 2026-09-26)

FX-26 sees the object model's bloblets (`docs/object-model.md`) as a type:

```
(bloblet (fields T …) R)    a bloblet in region R, fields of types T …
(bloblet (frozen T …) R)    the same, its fields frozen
```

The operations are syntax, since a field's type depends on its index, which
must be a literal. Field `i` is the program's `i`th, from 0; the object model
calls it `i + 2`, after the trailer.

| form                        | type                       | effect                        |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| `(make-bloblet bytes e …)`  | `(bloblet (fields T …) R)` | `(alloc R)`                   |
| `(bloblet-ref b i)`         | `Tᵢ`                       | `(read R)`, or pure if frozen |
| `(bloblet-set! b i e)`      | `unit`; not if frozen      | `(write R)`                   |
| `(bloblet-freeze b)`        | `(bloblet (frozen T …) R)` | `(write R)`                   |
| `(bloblet-byte b i)`        | `int`                      | `(read R)`                    |
| `(bloblet-set-byte! b i n)` | `unit`                     | `(write R)`                   |
| `(bloblet-bytes b)`         | `int`                      | pure                          |

`make-bloblet` takes `R` from the type it is checked against, or makes a
fresh region, as implicit projection does for `cons`. Mutable fields are
invariant; frozen fields covariant.

**A bloblet type lists at least its fields** (2026-10-10, `TODO.md` §77):
`(bloblet (fields T₀ … Tₙ …) R)` fits `(bloblet (fields T₀ … Tₙ) R)`,
since a field counts from the suffix and keeps its offset in a bloblet of
more fields; inference reads the fields a pattern lists. A field is
forgotten only if no analysis of the wider type needed it
(`Checker::forgettable`, `k-forgettable?`): of a frozen bloblet, data at
its place; of one not frozen, what mentions no region but `R` and frozen
data's in the heap, stores only in `R`, and holds no procedure, nor a type
variable that could be one. So the regions a type mentions (`letrena`'s
escape check, masking), its storage, whether it is data, and the knots a
store could tie are all in what it lists. An exact form waits for a need.
Tests: `bloblet/width*.fx`. Freezing changes the type and is never
a subtype, since another name for the same bloblet could still write it;
the runtime refuses that write, since the frozen flag is in the header.

**Flat data and `blobref`** (2026-10-10, `TODO.md` §77). The kind `flat`
is a layout of raw bytes in a bloblet's suffix: today the scalars `f64`,
`f32`, `i32`, `u32`, `i64`, `u64`, or a variable of the kind; products,
sums and arrays of flats to come. A flat is not a type (`` `f` is a flat,
not a type ``): a flat thing is no value, and need not fit a word. It is
reached through `(blobref B F)`, a pointer to an `F` in the suffix of a
bloblet of type `B`, its owner, which it keeps alive: standard generative
type 5, `(blobref (b type +) (f flat)) b`, so that to the safety analyses a
blobref is its owner (its regions, its storage), covariant in the owner
since the owner is only given out. At run time it is a suffix ref, tag
`010` (`docs/object-model.md`). Only a scalar's bytes come out, as a value
of the type of the same name.

| form                 | type                                       | effect                      |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| `(blobref-at b off)` | `(blobref B F)`, `F` the checked-against's | pure                        |
| `(blobref+ r d)`     | `(blobref B F)`, `F` the checked-against's | pure                        |
| `(blobref-owner r)`  | `B`                                        | pure                        |
| `(blobref-offset r)` | `int`                                      | pure                        |
| `(blobref-get r)`    | the scalar `F`'s type                      | `(read R)`, `R` the owner's |
| `(blobref-set! r v)` | `unit`; `v` of `F`'s type                  | `(write R)`                 |

Syntax, as the bloblet forms are: binders have no subtype bounds, so no
primitive's type could say "a bloblet type at region `R`"; the forms read
the owner's type itself, its region from it. A new blobref's flat is the
type it is checked against's (`(the (blobref B F) (blobref-at b 8))`), as a
new bloblet's region is. The offset is checked at run time (in the suffix,
and a read or write within it); a write into a frozen suffix fails. A new
blobref's flat lies whole in the suffix, checked where it is made
(`%fx26-suffix-fits`, the bytes the checker found it takes,
`Checker::note_fits`; of an array that runs to the end, none of its
elements): so what a blobref's type says of its flat, an array's length
above all, holds of the suffix wherever the blobref is. The
checker tells the compilers each read or write's layout (`Facts::field_index`,
as an `extract`'s field; the evaluator written in FX-26 reads the facts
too, its blobref an owner and an offset over its bloblets' arrays of
bytes). Natively (register code compiled by `fixpt-native`'s `direct.rs`),
a read, a write, a step (`blobref+`, an `extract` of one), and its owner
and offset are in line: the owner `ubfx` bits 3..37 of the ref, the
offset `lsr #37`, the suffix at the heap's base plus the owner times 8;
the owner's header found through its trailer, for the suffix's length (a
read or write within it, at any alignment), its kind and whether its
suffix is frozen (a write); what the inline path does not do (an owner
without a trailer, a 64-bit integer past a fixnum) is the primitive's. Tests: `bloblet/blobref*.fx`.

**Flat products and arrays.** Where a flat is wanted, `(productof (label
F) …)` lays its fields out in the order written, and `(arrayof F n)`, `n`
a literal, is `n` `F`s one after another (standard generative type 6,
`flat-arrayof`); in a type, an `arrayof` with a literal length is a flat
array, so `define-type` names one. Laid out as C does: each scalar aligned
to its size, a product padded to its widest member, an array's element as
its own. `(extract r l)` of a blobref to a flat product is a blobref to its
field `l`, at its offset; `(blobref-index r i)` of one to a flat array, one
to element `i`, checked against the length at run time. Neither copies a
byte: what comes out as a value is only ever one word, a scalar, by
`blobref-get` (the user's rule, 2026-10-10: an operation that copies more
than a word must say so in its name). Tests: `bloblet/flat-*.fx`,
`bloblet-errors/`.

**Flat arrays of a length not literal** (2026-10-12, `TODO.md` §77, with
the user). `(arrayof F n)`, `n` a size (a variable, or `finite`), runs to
the end of its owner's suffix: its length is what is left of the suffix,
in whole elements. So only a product's last part may be one (the product
then runs to the end too), and not an array's element nor a sum's
variant. A new blobref (`blobref-at`, `blobref+`) may claim one only of
length `finite`; binding the blobref to a name gives the length a size
variable of its own, named after it, as a `nat` of no known size is
given one (`name_nat`), so that what is learned of the length is of that
array. A blobref given back out of that scope forgets it, as `finite`
still truthfully; a procedure that takes an array of that length may not
leave it (F8). A size is solved from a generative type's argument, so
`(poly ((n size)) (subr … ((blobref B (arrayof f64 n))) …))` takes such
a blobref. In a type, `(arrayof T x)` is a flat array whenever `x` is
written as a size. `blobref-index` into one is checked against the end
of the suffix (`FLAT_TO_THE_END`). `(blobref-length r)` is a flat array's
length, a `(nat n)` of its length `n` (a literal's, or the whole
elements left of the suffix); a test of an index against it teaches
`i < n` (N5c's facts, `docs/research/sizes.md`), and an index so shown
within the length is recorded (`NodeFacts::in_bounds`): its check is
needless, and its debt discharged (`TODO.md` §86). The compilers still
make the check. A length named where such a blobref is bound means just
what `finite` does there, so a blobref of it is one of `finite` (only
inside a blobref, and only that length: a literal one is not), and a
procedure of one passes its own parameter on. Natively, `blobref-length`
is in line (a loop of a million `i64`s read, 9.4 → 3.2 ns an element).
Tests: `bloblet/flat-unsized*.fx`, `bloblet/flat-bounds.fx`,
`bloblet-errors/flat-unsized-range.fx`.

**Flat sums.** Where a flat is wanted, `(sumof (label F) …)` is a tag,
then the variant at the widest variant's alignment; its size is the tag's
and the widest variant's, padded to the alignment (`TODO.md` §77). With no
key, the tag is a byte indexing the owner's fields, which hold the labels
as symbols, so a flat sum's labels mean what a value sum's do: the program
puts them there (`(make-bloblet n 'circle 'square)`). With a key first,
`(sumof u8 (0 F) (7 G))` (or `u16`, `u32`), the labels are numbers below
the key's range, stored as they are, as Rust stores a discriminant
(standard generative type 7, `flat-keyed`); a label may now be 0.
`(blobref-variant! r 'l)` (a number, where the sum has a key) writes the
tag, the index of the first of the owner's fields that holds `l`, and
gives a blobref to the variant; no field holding it is an error at run
time. `tagcase` on a blobref to a flat sum reads the tag once and binds
each arm's name to a blobref to its variant (no arm takes a product
apart: nothing is copied); `else` binds the blobref as it is. The tag is
what the bytes say: one naming no field, or a label no arm has, fails at
run time, and since nothing keeps the owner's labels unique or unchanged,
a label in two fields reads the same through either, and a field
overwritten changes what every tag naming it means; both sound, since a
flat scalar has no invalid bits. Its effects: `tagcase` reads the owner's
region, `blobref-variant!` reads and writes it. Tests:
`bloblet/flat-sum*.fx`, `flat-variant-*.fx`, `flat-tagcase-*.fx`,
`bloblet-errors/flat-*.fx`, `native/flat-sums.fx`.

**Not yet: the `init` effect.** `make-bloblet` initialises every field in
one step, so no field is ever seen uninitialised, and the construction
protocol (reserve, zero, publish) stays inside the runtime. A typed
protocol is wanted once FX-26 code itself allocates across collections,
which is the compiler written in FX-26 (step 11); it comes with its first
user. Code-pointer types likewise.

## Fixed-width integers (2026-09-29)

`i32`, `u32`, `i64` and `u64` are integers of 32 or 64 bits, signed or
not, whose arithmetic **wraps** (the user's, 2026-09-29), for machine
words, hashing and bit operations; `u8` and `i8`, of 8 bits, for bytes
(since 2026-10-12, kayak's request, `TODO.md` §84). `int` is apart: an
exact integer of any size ("`int` is a bignum", below). The
operations are named by type (the user's choice, 2026-09-29, over a width
argument or overloading `+`); for each `T` of the six:

| operation                                                                              | type                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| `T+` `T-` `T*` `T-quotient` `T-remainder`                                              | `(subr pure (T T) T)`    |
| `T-and` `T-or` `T-xor`                                                                 | `(subr pure (T T) T)`    |
| `T-not`                                                                                | `(subr pure (T) T)`      |
| `T-shl` `T-shr` (arithmetic if signed, else logical; the count taken modulo the width) | `(subr pure (T int) T)`  |
| `T<` `T<=` `T>` `T>=` `T=`                                                             | `(subr pure (T T) bool)` |
| `int->T` (wraps)                                                                       | `(subr pure (int) T)`    |
| `T->int`                                                                               | `(subr pure (T) int)`    |

`T-quotient` truncates and `T-remainder` takes the dividend's sign; both
fail on a zero divisor.

A shift's count is any `int`, a bignum too, taken modulo the width as a
mathematical modulus: `(u32-shl 1 -1)` is `(u32-shl 1 31)`, and
`(u64-shl 1 (+ 64 2))` shifts by 2, on every machine and in native code
(kayak's report, 2026-10-11: a count past a fixnum had been refused by
the Rust machines, and native code shifted by the bignum's pointer).
`arithmetic-shift` by a count past a fixnum is `0` or `-1` rightwards (by
the sign of what is shifted), `0` for `0`, and else fails: the answer
would not fit in memory.

An `i8`, a `u8`, an `i32` or a `u32` is an `int`, the fixnum it stands for (subtyping,
2026-10-08, the user's): one goes wherever an `int` is wanted, and
`(+ x 1)` of a `u32` `x` is an `int`. So is an `i64` or a `u64` (since
2026-10-11, `DONE.md` §63), the exact integer it stands for, a bignum
past a fixnum; native code, which keeps one raw in a register, boxes it
where an `int` is wanted. And an integer literal is one where
any fixed-width type is expected and the literal is in its range
(`i64` and `u64` too since 2026-10-11, kayak's request): `(define h u32
2166136261)`, `(i64+ 1 2)`, `(define big u64 18446744073709551615)`.
One out of the range is refused, the range said: `(define y u32 -1)`,
"-1 is not a u32: its range is 0 to 4294967295". A literal past a fixnum
is written by the parsers as arithmetic on fixnums (no bignum is a
constant in code); the checkers read that back as the literal it is
(`Checker::literal_value`: the standard `+`, `*` and `-`, every node
spanning the literal), so arithmetic written out is still an `int`:
`(define y i64 (+ 1 2))` is refused. Where nothing is expected, a
literal is an `int`, as before.

At run time an `i32` or `u32` is the fixnum of its value (the user's
choice, over an immediate with a subtag), and an `i64` or `u64` the exact
integer it stands for (a bignum past 60 bits), which `T->int` gives.

The operations never collect (`fixpt_runtime::never_collects`), so
register code calls them with its values in registers (`prim1`, `prim2`,
`prim2imm`), and native code does each in a few instructions: an `i32` or
`u32` op is the fixnums' one, wrapped (`docs/performance.md`, "Primitives
that never collect, in line"); an `i64` or `u64` one is the machine's own,
on raw 64 bits. Native code keeps those raw in registers between
operations, a loop's variable raw around the loop, and boxes one only
where a value is read ("`i64` and `u64` raw in native registers").

```
(define fnv (subr pure (u32 u32) u32)
  (lambda (h c) (u32* (u32-xor h c) 16777619)))
```

## Bytevectors: `bytevectorof` (2026-10-12)

Bytes, for files and text (kayak's request, `TODO.md` §84):
`(bytevectorof r)`, generative type 8, a sequence of `u8`s made, read and
written at region `r`, as an array is. At run time it is the heap's
bytevector, the one a reader makes, its bytes raw; to the safety analyses
an array of `u8` at `r`, so a write says `(write r)` and one made at a
region that ends may not outlive it (`regions/bytevector-escapes.fx`). A
reader's bytevector, in a `datum`, is still the opaque `bytevector`. Its
operations are R7RS's, the region a binder of each:

| operation            | type                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `make-bytevector`    | `(subr (alloc r) (int u8) (bytevectorof r))`: `n` copies of a byte                                                      |
| `bytevector-length`  | `(subr pure ((bytevectorof r)) nat)`                                                                                    |
| `bytevector-u8-ref`  | `(subr (read r) ((bytevectorof r) int) u8)`                                                                             |
| `bytevector-u8-set!` | `(subr (write r) ((bytevectorof r) int u8) unit)`                                                                       |
| `bytevector-copy`    | `(subr (maxeff (read r1) (alloc r2)) ((bytevectorof r1) int int) (bytevectorof r2))`: start, end                        |
| `bytevector-copy!`   | `(subr (maxeff (read r1) (write r2)) ((bytevectorof r2) int (bytevectorof r1) int int) unit)`: to, at, from, start, end |
| `bytevector=?`       | `(subr (maxeff (read r1) (read r2)) ((bytevectorof r1) (bytevectorof r2)) bool)`                                        |
| `string->utf8`       | `(subr (alloc r) (string) (bytevectorof r))`                                                                            |
| `utf8->string`       | `(subr (read r) ((bytevectorof r)) (sumof (text string) (bad int)))`                                                    |

An index out of range, a span that does not fit, or a length below 0
fails the run, as an array's does. `utf8->string` of bytes that are not
UTF-8 is `(sum bad k)`, `k` the offset of the first byte that is not, a
value the program sees rather than a failure. Native code reads and
writes a byte, and the length, in line (2026-10-12: a sum over 4 MB of
bytes 5× faster, filling them 6×), the index checked; the rest are calls
of the runtime's primitives. Not yet: a bytevector in a place
(`rmake-bytevector`).
Tests: `run/bytevectors.fx`, `native/bytevector-sum.fx`,
`run/bytevector-printed.fx`.

## I/O: the `io` effects (2026-10-12)

A program touches the world outside it through primitives, each with an
effect from a fixed family (kayak's request, `TODO.md` §84), so a type
shows which procedures can, and in what way: `(io k)`, an atom beside
`read`, `write` and `alloc`, combined with `maxeff` as they are, `k` one
of

| effect          | covers                                    |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| `(io stdin)`    | reading the standard input                |
| `(io stdout)`   | writing the standard output               |
| `(io stderr)`   | writing the standard error                |
| `(io fs-read)`  | reading files and directories             |
| `(io fs-write)` | writing files                             |
| `(io clock)`    | reading the clock, and sleeping           |
| `(io args)`     | the command-line arguments                |
| `(io exit)`     | ending the program, `exit`                |
| `(io terminal)` | the terminal: its modes and size          |
| `(io native)`   | machine code the program made, run (§97)  |
| `(io sys)`      | a capability the runtime provides by name ("Syscalls") |
| `(io process)`  | programs run beside this one (Heist's F4) |

A procedure whose effect has none of them does no I/O. **One ordering
rule for all of them** (the user's, 2026-10-12): no two `io` effects
commute, and none is dropped, merged or moved, whatever their kinds, so a
write to the standard error never passes an earlier one to the standard
output. The kinds are for reading types and for licences, not for the
optimizer: to the compilers each is "anything else" (effect summary 2).
As `spin`, an `io` atom has no region, so nothing masks it; the REPL's
speculation never runs one (`licence.rs`).

| operation          | type                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `write-out`        | `(subr (io stdout) (string) (sumof (done unit) (failed io-error)))`                                                   |
| `write-err`        | `(subr (io stderr) (string) (sumof (done unit) (failed io-error)))`                                                   |
| `exit`             | `(subr (io exit) (int) void)`                                                                                         |
| `read-line`        | `(subr (io stdin) () (sumof (line string) (eof unit) (failed io-error)))`                                             |
| `read-byte`        | `(subr (io stdin) () (sumof (byte u8) (eof unit) (failed io-error)))`                                                 |
| `read-bytes`       | `(subr (maxeff (io stdin) (write r)) ((bytevectorof r) int int) (sumof (count int) (eof unit) (failed io-error)))`    |
| `stdin-ready?`     | `(subr (io stdin) () bool)`: whether a read would not wait                                                            |
| `clock`            | `(subr (io clock) () i64)`: monotonic nanoseconds since the run began                                                 |
| `sleep-until`      | `(subr (io clock) (i64) unit)`: until the clock reaches it                                                            |
| `args`             | `(subr (maxeff (io args) (alloc r)) () (listof string r))`                                                            |
| `read-file`        | `(subr (maxeff (io fs-read) (alloc r)) (string) (sumof (data (bytevectorof r)) (failed io-error)))`                   |
| `list-dir`         | `(subr (maxeff (io fs-read) (alloc r)) (string) (sumof (names (listof string r)) (failed io-error)))`                 |
| `write-file`       | `(subr (maxeff (io fs-write) (read r)) (string (bytevectorof r)) (sumof (done unit) (failed io-error)))`              |
| `terminal-raw`     | `(subr (io terminal) () (sumof (done unit) (not-a-terminal unit) (failed io-error)))`                                 |
| `terminal-restore` | `(subr (io terminal) () (sumof (done unit) (not-a-terminal unit) (failed io-error)))`                                 |
| `terminal-size`    | `(subr (io terminal) () (sumof (size (productof (rows int) (columns int))) (not-a-terminal unit) (failed io-error)))` |
| `process-start`    | `(subr (maxeff (io process) (read r)) ((listof string r) string) (sumof (started int) (failed io-error)))`: a program and its arguments, run in a directory (`""`: this one's), in a session of its own; its number |
| `process-output`   | `(subr (io process) (int) string)`: what it wrote (standard output and error, one stream) since last asked; never waits |
| `process-status`   | `(subr (io process) (int) (sumof (running unit) (exited int) (signalled int) (unknown unit)))`: running until it has exited and its output is all read |
| `process-write`    | `(subr (io process) (int string) (sumof (done unit) (failed io-error)))`: to its standard input |
| `process-close-input` | `(subr (io process) (int) unit)` |
| `process-signal`   | `(subr (io process) (int int) unit)`: a signal to its process group |

An I/O error is a value the program sees, not a failure of the run:
`io-error` is a standard type, `(productof (kind (sumof (not-found unit)
(permission-denied unit) (already-exists unit) (not-a-directory unit)
(is-a-directory unit) (broken-pipe unit) (invalid-data unit) (other
unit))) (detail string))`, the kind for a `tagcase`, the detail the
system's words. A write to a pipe whose reader has gone is `broken-pipe`.

**Running a program** (decided with the user, 2026-10-12): `fixpt run
FILE…` runs the files' forms, then, if the program defines `main`, calls
`(main)`. The status is `main`'s result's low byte if it is an integer,
else 0. A run that fails (a trap, `error`) exits 70, `EX_SOFTWARE`, its
message on the standard error. `(exit n)` ends the run at once with `n`'s
low byte, whatever is running. A program refused, or a file that cannot
be read, exits 1. A program run whole has no step budget, as the REPL's
forms do, unless `--step-limit` asks for one. `fixpt eval` performs I/O too, and ends with `exit`'s
status; otherwise as before. Writes are flushed as they are made, so
the two streams interleave as written. A path is as the working
directory resolves it. `list-dir` gives the names sorted by their bytes,
so its answer does not depend on the file system's order; a name that is
not UTF-8 is an `invalid-data` failure. `write-file` replaces a file whole
and atomically: written beside it, flushed to the disk, and renamed over
it, so a reader sees the old contents or the new. The region binder of
`args`, `read-file` and `list-dir` is where what they give is made.
`fixpt run FILE… -- ARG…` gives the program the ARGs, which `args`
returns. Tests: `tests/programs/io/` (the checkers),
`fixpt-cli/tests/io.rs` (each on five machines). `read-line` gives the
next line of the standard input with its ending (`\n` or `\r\n`) left
off, the last one even with none, then `eof`; a line not UTF-8 is an
`invalid-data` failure, and reading goes on after it. Later members of
the family (`TODO.md` §84): subprocesses, sockets, signals.

**Bytes, and the terminal** (kayak's request, 2026-10-14, `TODO.md`
§95), for a program that takes keys as they are typed, such as a front
end that edits a line. `read-byte` gives the next byte of the standard
input, waiting for one, or `eof`; `read-bytes bv start end` waits for one
byte, then takes as many as have come, at most `end - start`, into `bv`
from `start`, and gives how many, or `eof` (none asked for, 0 at once);
`stdin-ready?` says whether a read would not wait (a byte has come, or the
input is at its end). Reads by lines and by bytes take from one stream, so
they may be mixed. A process waits for a key as it waits for a message,
with a `pselect` arm `((read-byte) b body …)`: ready once a read would not
wait, its body then given what `read-byte` gives, so a front end waits for
a key, a message or a deadline, whichever comes first ("`pselect`",
below). When no task can run, the scheduler sleeps until the input is
ready or the earliest deadline, whichever is first.

`terminal-raw` puts the terminal the standard input is in raw mode: no
echo, no line editing, each key as it comes, ^C, ^Z and ^\ keys rather
than signals, a return not made a newline; what is written is as before
(a newline still begins the next line). `terminal-restore` puts back the
modes before raw mode. `terminal-size` gives its rows and columns. When the
standard input is no terminal (a pipe, a file, a test), each says so,
`not-a-terminal`, rather than failing, so the same program runs from a
script; a program that reads by bytes works the same there. Whatever the
program does, `fixpt` puts the terminal back as it found it when the run
ends, failed or not, and the REPL after each form: a pair of operations,
not a form around a body, since a failure in FX-26 unwinds nothing that a
form could run on the way, and a process that fails leaves the rest
running. A change of the terminal's size is found by asking again (a
front end asks as it redraws); as a message it would wait for signals, a
later member of the family.

## Processes and `par` (kayak's request, 2026-10-13)

Concurrency in two layers, sharing no mutable memory between processes
(`TODO.md` §92; the design and what it settles of the request,
`docs/research/processes.md`):
- **`par`**, deterministic parallelism: branches that may run at once,
  which the checker accepts only if no branch can see what another does.
- **Processes**, which share only what cannot be written: each has a
  mailbox, an address others send to, and a place of its own; messages
  are copied to it.

A program means the same whether its processes take turns under one
scheduler or run on threads of their own, each with a heap of its own:
no rule relies on turns, and a process never sees another's mutable data.
The one nondeterminism is said in the types, `nondet` (below).

### The forms

| form                       | what it is                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `(spawn (box M r) body …)` | a new process running `body …`, its value the process's address, `(addr M A)`; `box`, its mailbox, `(inbox M r)`; `r`, its place, a reap |
| `(send a m)`               | `m`, copied, to the process whose address is `a`: `(subr (send A) ((addr M A) M) unit)`                                                  |
| `(receive box)`            | the oldest message in the process's own mailbox, waiting for one: `(subr (maxeff (receive r) nondet) ((inbox M r)) M)`                   |
| `(self box)`               | the process's own address, at its place, from its mailbox: `(subr pure ((inbox M r)) (addr M r))`                                        |
| `(icell-get c)`            | I-cell `c`'s value, waiting for its one write: `(subr (await R) ((icell T R)) T)`                                                        |
| `(icell-put! c v)`         | `c`'s one write, which wakes whoever waits: `(subr (init R) ((icell T R) T) unit)`                                                       |
| `(par ((x e) …) body …)`   | each `e`, perhaps at once, two or more, then `body …` with their values bound, as `let` binds; checked for interference (below)          |
| `(pselect (W x body …) …)` | the arm whose wait (`icell-get`, `receive`, `sleep-until`, `read-byte`) is ready first, `x` its value (`pselect`, below)                 |
| `(race e …)`               | the value of a branch that finished, two or more, the others cancellable (`race`, below)                                                 |

All nine are forms, not procedures: they cannot be passed as values, and
the names are no longer free for a program's own (`scheme-bench/matrix.fx`
named its message procedure `send`, now `send-to`).

**Types and effects.** `(addr M R)` is the right to send an `M` to one
process's mailbox, in region `R`, which sending names, `(send R)`; it may
be copied, stored and sent freely. `(inbox M r)` is the right to take from
it: `spawn` binds it, at the process's own place `r`, and only there; a
message type is outside `r`'s scope, so it can never be sent. `(receive
r)` is taking a message. `(self box)` is the process's own address at its
place, `(addr M r)`: for sending itself messages, and giving to processes
it spawns, but not out of its place, which `r` names. `(init R)` is an I-cell's fill (FX's `init`):
within `(write R)` for every purpose but `par`'s, where it commutes with
reads and awaits, which wait for it. `nondet` is an atom with no region,
as `spin` is, which no masking removes: an expression whose answer may
differ from run to run says so.

### What a process may hold: transmissible types

A process's body crosses into the process, and each message crosses into
its receiver, so each must be **transmissible**:
- numbers, characters, booleans, strings, symbols, `unit`, `nil`;
- products, sums and unions of transmissible types;
- data frozen in the heap (at `const` or `acyclic`): pairs, lists,
  arrays, references and bloblets; not data frozen into a place (`(const
  p)`), which ends with the place;
- addresses, `(addr M R)` (shared), and I-cells of transmissible values,
  `(icell T R)` (shared: a capability, which is how a reply comes back,
  and always in the heap, wherever it was asked for, so no process holds
  one in a place that ended);
- procedures whose parameters and result are transmissible and whose
  latent effect does nothing a process may not do anywhere: send, fill or
  await an I-cell, I/O, `spin`, `nondet`, read globals' bindings or
  frozen data, allocate at a region that is no place (a constant, or the
  heap), or anything on a binder of its own `poly`;
- a type variable of kind `sendable`, of kind `data` in the heap, or one
  a `poly` around binds.

**The `sendable` kind** (`data ≤ sendable ≤ type`, 2026-10-14): a binder
`(m sendable)` takes only a transmissible type, so that a server may be
polymorphic in its messages. Of kind `type`, `m` could be anything, and a
`spawn` whose message type holds it is refused; of kind `sendable`, it is
transmissible, and each instantiation is checked to be:

```
(define-type (req (m type)) (productof (v m) (reply (icell m @r))))
(define* answer (poly ((m sendable)) (subr (init @r) ((req m)) unit))
  (plambda ((m sendable)) (lambda (q) (icell-put! (extract q reply) (extract q v)))))
(define* make-echo
  (poly ((m sendable))
    (subr (maxeff (alloc @e) (init @r) nondet spin) () (addr (req m) @e)))
  (plambda ((m sendable))
    (lambda ()
      (spawn (box (req m) p)
        (letrec ((go (subr (maxeff (receive p) (init @r) nondet spin) () unit)
                   (lambda () (begin ((proj answer m) (receive box)) (go)))))
          (go))))))
(define echo (addr (req int) @e) ((proj make-echo int)))

((proj make-echo (ref int @c)))
;; ! `m` is bound as sendable, and a (ref int @c) is not: a reference at
;;   @c, which can be written
```

Not a reference, array, pair or bloblet that can be written, a mailbox
but as a parameter of a procedure polymorphic in its place, a tag, a key, a
continuation, a place, a module, or a generative or abstract type.

**`spawn`'s rule.** The body is checked as `(plambda ((r place)) (lambda
((box (inbox M r)) (r (place r))) body … #u))`. Then each of its free
variables, globals included (but not the standard bindings, nor the hidden
globals of loads), must be of a transmissible type; `M` must be; and its
latent effect, without what is on `r`, must be only what a process may do
(the list above). That rest is the spawn's effect, with `nondet` added if
it does I/O (two processes' output interleaves as it will) and `(alloc A)`
for the address. The address's region `A` is the one expected (`(the
(addr M @srv) (spawn …))`), else one of its own.

```
(define-type req (productof (n int) (reply (icell int @r))))
(define doubler (addr req @srv)
  (spawn (box req p)
    (letrec ((serve (subr (maxeff (receive p) (init @r) nondet spin) () unit)
               (lambda ()
                 (let ((m (receive box)))
                   (begin (icell-put! (extract m reply) (* 2 (extract m n))) (serve))))))
      (serve))))
;; doubler : (addr req @srv) ! (maxeff (alloc @srv) (init @r) nondet spin)
(define* ask (subr (maxeff (send @srv) (alloc @r) (await @r)) (int) int)
  (lambda (n)
    (let ((c (the (icell int @r) (make-icell))))
      (begin (send doubler (product (n n) (reply c))) (icell-get c)))))
(+ (ask 20) (ask 1))   ; 42
```

Refused (`tests/programs/processes/`):

```
(define counter (ref int @c) (new 0))
(define w (addr int @w) (spawn (box int p) (set counter (receive box))))
;; ! a process may share nothing that can be written, and its body holds
;;   `counter`: a reference at @c, which can be written

(define w (addr (ref int @c) @w) (spawn (box (ref int @c) p) (set (receive box) 1)))
;; ! a process's messages are copied to it, so they must be transmissible,
;;   and a (ref int @c) is not: a reference at @c, which can be written

(define* start (subr (alloc @w) () (addr int @w))
  (lambda () (spawn (box int p) (begin (receive box) #u))))
;; ! … beyond what is expected, it has nondet
```

**`nondet`.** A process that receives may see its messages in any order
its senders' sends allow, so `receive` says `nondet`, and so does a
procedure that calls it, and a `spawn` of a body that does. Masking never
removes it: a function that spawns a receiving process privately is not
pure. What a function learns from a process it did not make shows another
way, in its unmasked `(send R)`. So a computation whose effect has
neither `nondet`, nor a send or an I-cell's await on a region outside it,
gives the same answer every run. A process that only computes and fills a
reply cell says no `nondet`, and gives one answer.

### The process's place

`r` in `(spawn (box M r) …)` is a place: a reap, entered for the process
when it starts and ended when it ends, the process's own heap (`rcons r
…`). Every task has its own stack of places (Q1, `DONE.md` §92), so a
process may wait (`receive`, `icell-get`) inside its own `letrena` or
`letreap`, while others enter and end theirs: neither `receive` nor an
await is a `comefrom`, and nothing that waits is ever resumed twice or
dropped while its places live. `programs/processes/own-place.fx` keeps two
processes' reaps open across interleaved receives.

### `par`

`(par ((x1 e1) … (xn en)) body …)` runs the branches as tasks, which may
run at once, and binds their values for `body …`. Its effect is every
branch's. No branch may transfer control out of itself (`goto`,
`comefrom`), and no two may **interfere**: on regions that may be one
(`regions_distinct`, Lucassen's rule for region variables),

| one \ other | read | write | alloc | init | await | send | receive | I/O |
| ----------- | ---- | ----- | ----- | ---- | ----- | ---- | ------- | --- |
| **read**    | ok   | X     | ok    | ok   | ok    | ok   | ok      | ok  |
| **write**   | X    | X     | ok    | X    | X     | ok   | ok      | ok  |
| **alloc**   | ok   | ok    | ok    | ok   | ok    | ok   | ok      | ok  |
| **init**    | ok   | X     | ok    | ok   | ok    | ok   | ok      | ok  |
| **await**   | ok   | X     | ok    | ok   | ok    | ok   | ok      | ok  |
| **send**    | ok   | ok    | ok    | ok   | ok    | X    | X       | ok  |
| **receive** | ok   | ok    | ok    | ok   | ok    | X    | X       | ok  |
| **I/O**     | ok   | ok    | ok    | ok   | ok    | ok   | ok      | X   |

and an effect variable, which may be any of them, against anything but
allocation, `spin` and `nondet`. Branches may read data no branch writes;
one may fill an I-cell another awaits (dataflow). So the answer is the
same however they interleave:

```
(define xs (arrayof int @x) (make-array 3 0))
(par ((a (begin (array-set! xs 0 1) 0)) (b (array-ref xs 0))) (+ a b))
;; ! `par`'s branches interfere: one has (write @x) and another (read @x),
;;   so the answer could depend on which runs first
```

### `pselect`

`(pselect (W x body …) …)` waits until one of its arms' waits is ready
and runs that arm: each `W` is `(icell-get c)`, ready once `c` is filled,
`(receive box)`, ready once the mailbox has a message, `(sleep-until
t)`, a deadline, ready once the clock (`clock`) reaches `t`, or
`(read-byte)`, a key, ready once a read of the standard input would not
wait (its effect `(io stdin)`; "I/O", above); and `x` is bound to what it
gets (unit, for a deadline; what `read-byte` gives, for a key). As Tokio's `select!` over futures that only wait:
the arms not chosen have done nothing, so dropping them is free (a
message is left in the mailbox). When several are ready, the first in
the arms' order is chosen; which are ready when the task looks is the
schedule's, so a `pselect` has `nondet` as well as its arms' `await`s and
`receive`s. (Named `pselect`, not `select`, which in a type or effect is
a module's component.)

```
(define stop (icell unit @c) (make-icell))
(define total (icell int @c) (make-icell))
(define summer (addr int @p)
  (spawn (box int r)
    (letrec ((loop (subr (maxeff (receive r) (await @c) (init @c) nondet spin) (int) unit)
               (lambda (n)
                 (pselect ((receive box) m (loop (+ n m)))
                          ((icell-get stop) u (icell-put! total n))))))
      (loop 0))))
(send summer 10)
(send summer 20)
(send summer 30)
(icell-put! stop #u)
(icell-get total)
;; => 60
```

A deadline arm gives a wait a time limit; its effect is `(io clock)`.
While a task waits for a deadline, the others run; when none can, the
scheduler sleeps until the earliest deadline any task waits for. So a
`pselect` of a deadline alone is a sleep that lets the other tasks run,
where `sleep-until` stops them all:

```
(define answer (icell int @c) (make-icell))
(define waiter (addr int @p)
  (spawn (box int r)
    (pselect ((receive box) m (icell-put! answer m))
             ((sleep-until (i64+ (clock) (int->i64 5000000))) u (icell-put! answer -1)))))
(icell-get answer)
;; => -1
```

A `pselect` nothing is left to make ready, no deadline among its arms or
any other task's, is a deadlock, as a wait is. Its expansion calls the
scheduler's events (generative type `(event e)`, `check::EVENT`):
`cell-event`, `mailbox-event`, `clock-event`, `stdin-event`, `or-event`
and `sync`, which
gives the chosen arm's number. A task that waits gives every event one
waker, which runs only the first time, and is taken back from all of
them once it has: a loop that waits on one cell many times leaves no
waker behind, nor a deadline passed over.

### `race`

`(race e1 … en)` runs its branches as tasks, which may run at once, and
gives the value of one that finished, as Tokio's `select!` over futures
that compute. Which finishes first is the schedule's, so a `race` has
`nondet`, and every branch's effect. Its branches give one type (the
wider of two, where one is a subtype of the other).

The branches that lose may be stopped at once, at their next wait, or
never: an implementation may choose, and FX-26's stops each at once if it
waits, its waker taken back, else at its next wait or preemption, or
before it starts. A branch that fails (`error`, a
trap) is lost, not the race's failure; a race whose branches all fail
fails as the last did. So that no program can tell which, each branch must be
**cancellable**: it may read, allocate, await, receive and spin, and do
anything on regions of its own (which masking leaves out of its effect),
but not write, fill an I-cell, send, do I/O, or transfer control out of
itself, nor have an effect not known (a variable). A branch stopped half
way and one run to its end then look the same. And no two may interfere,
as `par`'s may not: two receives from one mailbox. Only termination
differs between implementations: a `race` may fail to end if a loser does
not, where one that stops losers promptly ends whenever any branch does.

```
(define a (icell int @c) (make-icell))
(define b (icell int @c) (make-icell))
(define w (addr int @p) (spawn (box int r) (icell-put! a (receive box))))
(send w 7)
(race (icell-get a) (icell-get b))
;; => 7

(define x (ref int @x) (new 0))
(race (begin (set x 1) 1) 2)
;; ! a `race` branch may be stopped half way, so it must do nothing another
;;   could see, and this one has (write @x)
```

Its expansion is nested `race2`s of thunks, as `par`'s is of `par2`s; the
scheduler cancels a loser that has not finished (`%sched`'s operation
24), which ends instead of running the next time it would.

### `attempt`

(Heist's, 2026-10-25: `FIXPT-CHANGES.md` F1.) `(attempt e on-failure)`
is `e`'s value, but should `e` fail (`error`, or a trap: `car` of `nil`,
an index out of range, a division by zero, a stack overflow), it is
`(on-failure message)` instead, and the program goes on. `on-failure`
is a `(subr E (string) T)`; `message` is what the failure says, as the
machine says it (`error`'s string as given). Its value is the wider of
`e`'s type and `on-failure`'s result, as `race`'s is, and its effect
both theirs and `nondet`: what a failure says, and whether a deep
recursion overflows, is the machine's.

`e` must be **cancellable**, as a `race` branch must: it may read,
allocate, await, receive and spin, and do anything on regions of its own,
but not write, fill an I-cell, send, do I/O, or transfer control out of
itself. So a failure half way leaves nothing another could see, and
`error` stays what §86 made it, never caught where a write could be half
done: `attempt` is a task's boundary, as a monitored process is, but in
place, its value a value rather than a message. `on-failure` runs after,
in the task that ran the `attempt`, and may do anything.

```
(define* first (subr (read @h) ((listof int @h)) int) (lambda (l) (car l)))
(attempt (first nil) (lambda ((m string)) 0))
;; => 0
(define x (ref int @x) (new 0))
(attempt (begin (set x 1) 1) (lambda ((m string)) 2))
;; ! an `attempt`'s body may be stopped half way, so it must do nothing
;;   another could see, and this one has (write @x)
```

Not running out of fuel (the step limit is the form's), as for monitors.
`e` runs as a task, a monitored group of its own as a `race` branch's is
(`attempt1`, `fork-race-branch`): about 1.7 µs a call natively, half of
them failing. Tests: `programs/attempt` (every way, and on each machine,
`fixpt-cli/tests/preempt.rs`).

### Monitors

`(spawn-monitored w on-failure (box M r) body …)` is `spawn`, but should
the process fail (`error`, or a trap: a division by zero, `car` of `nil`,
a stack overflow), the form it runs in does not: its **watcher**, the
address `w`, `(addr W R)`, is sent `(on-failure message)`, `on-failure` a
`(subr pure (string) W)` and `message` what the failure says. Its effect
is `spawn`'s and `(send R)`. For a long-lived process that hands each
piece of work to a worker and reports a failure rather than failing with
it (kayak's request, `TODO.md` §94):

```
(define-type report (sumof (ok int) (failed string)))
(define* failed (subr pure (string) report) (lambda (m) (sum failed m)))
(define* work (subr spin (int) int) (lambda (k) (if (= k 0) (error "bad input") k)))
;; `front`, the front end's address, a process whose mailbox takes reports:
(spawn-monitored front failed (box int r) (send front (sum ok (work (receive box)))))
;; sent 0, the worker fails, and the front end receives (failed "bad input")
```

A monitored process is a group: its tasks, the branches of its `par`s and
`race`s, are its, and a failure in any is the process's; the rest of the
group is cancelled, and the watcher is sent one message. What a failure
leaves half done needs no cleaning: a process shares only what cannot be
written, and its place, and every task's, ends with it. Not running out of
fuel (the step limit is the form's), nor a failure in the program's own
computation, which fails the form as before. A process that ends normally
sends nothing; a watcher that has ended has the message in a mailbox no
one reads. `par`'s branches keep failing as the `par` (the process, or the
form); `race`'s are lost (above). The message is the failure's text, as
the machine says it: `error`'s string as given.

Each machine, at a failure in a task, asks the runtime
(`fixpt_runtime::sched::task_failed`); in a monitored group, the failure
becomes an abort to the scheduler's prompt, as a task that waits leaves,
and the scheduler gives the group's handler the message (for a monitored
process, a send to the watcher; for a `race` branch, it is lost). A
`race` branch's group is inside the group of the task that runs the race,
and a failure cancels its group and every group inside it. Tests:
`programs/monitors` (every way, and on each machine,
`fixpt-cli/tests/preempt.rs`).

### Running

The scheduler is FX-26, `fx26:processes.fx`, a module file built in that
the forms call (its procedures' types as the forms see them are the
checkers', `%processes:` in the standard table, which no program names);
it runs every way FX-26 runs. A task waits by capturing the rest of
itself up to the scheduler's prompt. The program's own computation runs
the others: when it waits, until what it waits for is ready (sleeping
until the earliest deadline when none can run and a task waits for one),
and after each `spawn`, `send` and `icell-put!` it makes, until none can
run but those preempted, so a top-level form ends with every task that can
run having run, or having been preempted (the end of a form is where a
definition may change a global, which no running process then sees half
done). A process waiting for ever (a server, when its clients are done) is
no error. The program waiting with nothing left to run is a **deadlock**,
an error that says so, not a hang:

```
;; ! an i-cell read before it was written, and no task is left to write it: a deadlock
```

**Preemption** (2026-10-14; the actors note's Q2). Tasks run in turn, and
a task that runs for long is preempted, on every machine: each machine's
fuel is given out in time slices (2^16 polls: bytecode instructions,
cells, procedure entries and loops' back edges), and at a slice's end, if
another task could run, the machine calls the scheduler's `preempt` where
it is. A task preempted is parked, apart from the run queue, and goes on
when the program waits or is itself preempted; the program's own task,
preempted, runs one other. So a process that spins for ever starves none:

```
(define answer (icell int @c) (make-icell))
(define* spin-forever (subr spin (int) int) (lambda (n) (spin-forever (+ n 1))))
(define spinner (addr int @p) (spawn (box int r) (begin (spin-forever (receive box)) #u)))
(define doubler (addr int @p) (spawn (box int r) (icell-put! answer (* 2 (receive box)))))
(send spinner 0)
(send doubler 21)
(icell-get answer)
;; => 42
```

Nothing a program can see depends on where a task is preempted: what
tasks share is immutable, `par`'s branches do not interfere, and `race`'s
are cancellable. `FIXPT_SLICE=n` makes slices of `n` polls, for testing.

A trap in any process fails the run of the form it was in; the processes
are then forgotten. Messages are copied (`Heap::copy_within`): data
deeply, I-cells, addresses and procedures shared. Tests:
`tests/processes.rs` (every program of `programs/processes` every way it
runs, the answers under twenty orders of the run queue, arrival order seen
to vary, the interference table cell by cell in both checkers, a failing
process), `fixpt-cli/tests/preempt.rs` (preemption on each machine, at
tiny slices too), and `fixpt-heap`'s per-task places.

## Code a program makes (kayak's request, 2026-10-14)

A program may write machine code into a bloblet's suffix, seal it, and
make a procedure of it, at a type it states (`TODO.md` §97; the design,
and the contract the code keeps, `docs/research/declared-conventions.md`):

| operation          | type                                                  |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| `seal-code`        | `(poly ((a type)) (subr (io native) (a) a))`          |
| `native-procedure` | `(poly ((a type) (t type)) (subr (io native) (a) t))` |
| `native-code?`     | `(subr pure () bool)`                                 |

`(seal-code b)` is a copy of bloblet `b` in the code area, its suffix
frozen and made runnable; `(the T (native-procedure c))` a native closure
of sealed copy `c`, of type `T`, trusted: nothing checks machine code
against a type, so both carry `(io native)`. `T` is a procedure type of
the native convention, `(subr (conv native) …)`, or `fx`; any other is
refused. The code is the machine's own, arm64 or x86_64, to the
convention `(native-convention)` says (`native-arm64.conv`,
`native-x86_64.conv`). Where `native-code?` is false (the evaluator
written in FX-26, and any other instruction set), both fail, and a
program interprets instead. Tests: `programs/made-code/leaves.fx`, and
for x86_64 `programs/made-code-x86_64/`.

**`(define-convention name clause …)`**, at top level, names the
convention code is made to by declaring it: each clause a role and where
it is (registers, the value layout, frames, the state, routines, the
write barrier). It is accepted when its clauses, in any order, are those
of the native convention, which `(native-convention)` gives, one clause to
a string, as `fixpt` generates them from its own constants; `name` is then
`native`, as in `(subr (conv name) …)`. Anything else is refused at the
first clause, by role, that differs. Test: `programs/made-code/declared.fx`.

## Syscalls (kayak's request, 2026-10-26)

One extension through which a runtime written in FX-26 calls what its
runtime provides, by name, at a type both sides state: so each runtime adds
capabilities without a change to the language (`docs/research/syscalls.md`,
the design and what Fixpt changed of it).

```
(define-syscall name "host-name" type)
(define-optional-syscall name "host-name" type)
(syscall-available? name)
```

- **Only a runtime's own code declares them**: a declaration is accepted in
  a module file built in, or in a program whose driver was told it is a
  runtime's (`--syscalls`); anywhere else it is refused, "syscalls are
  declared only by a runtime's own code".
- **`host-name`** is `namespace:name/version`: the namespace and the name
  of lower-case letters, digits and `-`, the version a number from 1. A
  name's type never changes; a new type is a new version.
- **`type`** is a `subr`, or a `poly` over regions only of one, of the
  program's own convention and no specification; its effect holds
  `(io sys)`, and only reads, writes and allocations of the `poly`'s
  regions, other `io` effects, `spin` and `nondet`; it takes at most six
  arguments; each argument and its result one of `int`, `i64`, `u64`,
  `bool`, `char`, `unit`, `string`, `(flatarrayof i64 r)` and
  `(bytevectorof r)`, `r` a region of the `poly`.
- **`name`** is defined, as `define` defines, to a procedure of that type;
  a call of it calls the runtime.
- **`(syscall-available? name)`**, `name` a syscall declared, is a `bool`:
  whether the runtime has it, at that type. Always `#t` for a required
  one, which loads only if it does.

**The canonical form of a type.** The declared type and the runtime's are
compared as text, so that a runtime needs no type checker: each is printed
in one form, which every implementation prints alike.

1. Every name a `define-type` (or a family) introduced is expanded to what
   it names.
2. An effect's atoms (a `maxeff` flattened, named effects expanded) are
   printed each once, sorted by their text, byte by byte; one alone is
   printed alone, more in a `maxeff`.
3. A `poly`'s region binders are renamed `r1`, `r2`, … in its binder
   list's order, and every use with them.
4. Atoms are apart by one space, with no other whitespace: `(read r1)`,
   `(io sys)`, `(flatarrayof i64 r1)`.

So `(poly ((zeta region) (alpha region)) (subr (maxeff (write alpha) sys
(read zeta) (read zeta)) ((words zeta) (words alpha) int) unit))`, with
`(define-type (words (r region)) (flatarrayof i64 r))` and
`(define-effect sys (io sys))`, is, canonically,

```
(poly ((r1 region) (r2 region)) (subr (maxeff (io sys) (read r1) (write r2)) ((flatarrayof i64 r1) (flatarrayof i64 r2) int) unit))
```

Equality only: no subtyping. A program that wants a looser type wraps the
syscall in a procedure of that type.

**What a declaration is.** Checked alone, never against a runtime; then,
its type `T` printed `TEXT`, `LABEL` being `syscall NAME ("HOST")`, it is
the two definitions

```
(define %syscall-available:NAME bool (%syscall-available? HOST TEXT))
(define NAME T (lambda ((x1 P1) …) (the R (%syscallN LABEL HOST TEXT x1 …))))
```

each part of `T` the canonical form's, and for a required one the first
`(begin (%syscall-require LABEL HOST TEXT) #t)`. `(syscall-available?
NAME)` is the first's name. `%syscall-require` and `%syscall0` to
`%syscall6` only a module file built in, or such a definition, may name;
`%syscall-available?` only asks, and anyone may. The first definition is
the check at load, which runs as the declaration does, before any form
after it:

- a required one the runtime has not: `syscall NAME ("HOST"): this runtime
  has none`;
- one it has at another type: `syscall NAME ("HOST"): declared TYPE; this
  runtime's is TYPE`, both canonical;
- a call of an optional one it has not: `syscall NAME ("HOST"): not
  available on this runtime`.

## `int` is a bignum (2026-09-30)

An `int` is an exact integer of any size (PLAN.md, Q2): a fixnum while it
fits 61 bits, a bignum past that, alike on every path. `+`, `-`, `*` and
`quotient` promote where they would overflow; `=`, `<` and the rest
compare by value; `modulo` takes bignums; a result that fits a fixnum is
one. The checkers are unchanged: `int` was always "an integer".

On every machine the fixnums' case is in line and anything else calls
the runtime's `num::int_op` (the Scheme engine's bignums, which the
lowered program uses too), with no collection: a fixnum's add is a test
of both operands' tags, the add, and a branch on overflow. `=` of ints
compiles to the routine `int-eq`, not `eq`: two bignums of one value are
two objects. A literal past a fixnum (2026-09-30) is read as arithmetic on
fixnums, the same in both parsers: `(- 0 x)` for a negative, and in base
10⁹ `(+ (* rest 1000000000) last)`, `rest` again so until it is a fixnum.
So every path makes the same bignum with no new kind of literal, and such
a literal is an `int`, not a `(nat k)`.

## Floats: `f64` (2026-09-30)

`f64` is IEEE binary64 (the user's name, 2026-09-30; `f32`, binary32, is
next). Literals are written with a point or an exponent: `2.`, `1.5`,
`.5`, `1e10`; `2` is an `int`, never an `f64`, and there is no
conversion but `int->f64`. The operations are named by type, as the
fixed-width integers' are, and are `pure`: IEEE's round to nearest even,
nothing trapping (`(f64/ 1. 0.)` is `+inf.0`).

| operation                                                                                          | type                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `f64+` `f64-` `f64*` `f64/` `f64-min` `f64-max` `f64-atan2` `f64-expt`                             | `(subr pure (f64 f64) f64)`                                    |
| `f64-abs` `f64-neg` `f64-sqrt` `f64-floor` `f64-ceiling` `f64-truncate` `f64-round` (ties to even) | `(subr pure (f64) f64)`                                        |
| `f64-exp` `f64-log` `f64-sin` `f64-cos` `f64-tan` `f64-asin` `f64-acos` `f64-atan`                 | `(subr pure (f64) f64)`                                        |
| `f64<` `f64<=` `f64>` `f64>=` `f64=` (false when either is a NaN)                                  | `(subr pure (f64 f64) bool)`                                   |
| `f64-nan?` `f64-infinite?` `f64-finite?`                                                           | `(subr pure (f64) bool)`                                       |
| `int->f64` (correctly rounded, of any `int`)                                                       | `(subr pure (int) f64)`                                        |
| `f64->int` (fails unless integral; a bignum past a fixnum)                                         | `(subr pure (f64) int)`                                        |
| `f64->string` (shortest that reads back)                                                           | `(subr pure (f64) string)`                                     |
| `string->f64` (correctly rounded; a list of it, or none)                                           | `(poly ((r region)) (subr (alloc r) (string) (listof f64 r)))` |

An `f64` is the heap's boxed flonum everywhere, the lowered program's
too; native code keeps one raw in registers (as `i64` and `u64`), each
operation the machine does exactly as the runtime does (`+ - * /`, `abs`,
`neg`, `sqrt`, the roundings, the comparisons) moved through a `d`
register, and boxes it where a value is read. `min`, `max` and the
elementary functions call the runtime, so that every machine gives the
same bits.

### `f32` (2026-09-30)

`f32` is IEEE binary32, rounded at each operation, as the machine does
it. At run time it is an immediate of its own (the user's choice, over
the fixnum shape `i32` has): its 32 bits in the word's upper half, under
the immediate tag with subtag 9, so that it allocates nothing, describes
itself to the printer, and boxes and unboxes by a shift. There are no
`f32` literals: `(f64->f32 .1)`, `(int->f32 3)`.

| operation                                                                           | type                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| `f32+` `f32-` `f32*` `f32/` `f32-min` `f32-max`                                     | `(subr pure (f32 f32) f32)`  |
| `f32-abs` `f32-neg` `f32-sqrt` `f32-floor` `f32-ceiling` `f32-truncate` `f32-round` | `(subr pure (f32) f32)`      |
| `f32<` `f32<=` `f32>` `f32>=` `f32=`                                                | `(subr pure (f32 f32) bool)` |
| `f32-nan?` `f32-infinite?` `f32-finite?`                                            | `(subr pure (f32) bool)`     |
| `int->f32` (correctly rounded, not through an `f64`), `f64->f32` (rounds)           | `(subr pure (int) f32)`, …   |
| `f32->int` (fails unless integral), `f32->f64` (exact), `f32->string`               | `(subr pure (f32) int)`, …   |
| `f32->bits`, `bits->f32`; `f64->bits`, `bits->f64` (a float's bits, and back)       | `(subr pure (f32) u32)`, …   |

The elementary functions are `f64`'s only: `sinf` is not the `f32` of
`sin`, so a program converts, and says so. Native code does the
arithmetic, roundings and comparisons in line, in `s16` and `s17`.

## Flat arrays: `flatarrayof` (2026-09-30)

`(flatarrayof T R)` is an array in region `R` whose elements are kept
raw, 4 or 8 bytes each, for `T` one of `f64`, `f32`, `i32`, `u32`, `i64`,
`u64` (the user's name, and Q6). No collection scans the elements and no
store marks a card. It is the third standard generative type (2), over
`(arrayof T R)`: its regions are an array's to the safety analyses, and
nothing else sees inside it.

A flat array says at run time what it holds, so its operations are
polymorphic in `T`; only making one needs to know, and takes a *layout*,
of type `(flatlayout T)` (generative type 1, over `int`), which only the
flat types have: dictionary passing, not a copy per type (decision 4).

| operation                                                         | type                                                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `f64-flat` `f32-flat` `i32-flat` `u32-flat` `i64-flat` `u64-flat` | `(subr pure () (flatlayout T))`, `T` as named                                                     |
| `make-flatarray`                                                  | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (alloc r) ((flatlayout t) int t) (flatarrayof t r))))` |
| `flatarray-ref`                                                   | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (read r) ((flatarrayof t r) int) t)))`                 |
| `flatarray-set!`                                                  | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (subr (write r) ((flatarrayof t r) int t) unit)))`           |
| `flatarray-length`                                                | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (subr pure ((flatarrayof t r)) nat)))`                       |

```
(make-flatarray (f64-flat) 1000 0.)   ; a thousand f64s, raw
```

At run time a flat array is a bloblet of kind `flat-array`: its layout in
field 2, its elements in the suffix. Natively, reading and writing are in
line, by the layout; an `f64` element that only `f64` operations use is
read raw, and one computed raw is stored as it is, so a loop over a flat
array of floats allocates nothing. Whether products and sums of flat data
may be flattened too (unboxed data types, as Rust has them) is a question
for later.

## Identity: `eq?`, and `eqtable` (2026-09-30)

`eq?`, of type `(poly ((t type)) (subr pure (t t) bool))`, is whether two
values are the same object (PLAN.md Q5, `DONE.md` §19; the user's
choice). It is exact on mutable objects (pairs and bloblets at a region,
refs, arrays, i-cells), which no compiler copies or merges, and on
atoms (fixnums, characters, booleans, symbols). On immutable data
(products, sums, frozen lists and bloblets, bignums, boxed floats) and on
procedures, the compilers may share, copy or rebuild what they like:
making constant data once, `apply` sharing an `acyclic` list (F11),
lambda lifting and specialization. There `#t` means the two are equal and
`#f` says nothing, as OCaml's `==` promises on immutable values and as
R6RS lets `eqv?` answer of procedures. So programs whose answers depend
on `eq?` of immutable data may differ between machines, and the tests do
not ask it.

It is `pure`: an object's identity never changes, so any one answer keeps
the promise wherever a compiler moves or repeats the test. Its type says
all a type can: the rest is the promise, not something an intersection of
types would add. To refuse it on procedures instead would take a kind of
types with equality, as SML's `''a`; not done. In every machine it is
`eq`, in line; the evaluator written in FX-26 compares its values' objects
(`ev-eq?`), and says `#f` of its products, sums and procedures.

(A first version, earlier the same day, had one test per kind of mutable
object, each a write of its region, so that the frozen-write rule kept
identity from immutable data; it could not reach bloblets, whose field
lists no type binder ranges over, nor a bloblet frozen in a region that is
not.)

**Tables keyed by identity.** `(eqtable k v kr r)`, generative type 4: keys
`k` whose region is `kr`, values `v`, the table in `r`. It is made with a
dictionary that says `k` has identity, `(identity k kr)` (generative type
3), which only the standard procedures make, and its operations write the
keys' region, so that no table is keyed by frozen data, whose lookups
would depend on what the compilers shared:

| operation                                                        | type                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `pair-identity` `ref-identity` `array-identity` `icell-identity` | `(subr pure () (identity K r))`, `K` the kind's type at `r`                    |
| `make-eqtable`                                                   | `(subr (alloc r) ((identity k kr)) (eqtable k v kr r))`                        |
| `eqtable-ref`                                                    | `(subr (maxeff (read r) (write kr)) ((eqtable k v kr r) k v) v)`, `v` if none  |
| `eqtable-has?`                                                   | `(subr (maxeff (read r) (write kr)) ((eqtable k v kr r) k) bool)`              |
| `eqtable-set!`                                                   | `(subr (maxeff (write r) (alloc r) (write kr)) ((eqtable k v kr r) k v) unit)` |
| `eqtable-delete!`                                                | `(subr (maxeff (write r) (write kr)) ((eqtable k v kr r) k) unit)`             |
| `eqtable-count`                                                  | `(subr (read r) ((eqtable k v kr r)) int)`                                     |

(each polymorphic in `kr`, `r`, `k` and `v`.)

```
(the (eqtable (pairof int int @k) int @k @t) (make-eqtable (pair-identity)))
```

A table hashes its keys by address (`fixpt_runtime::eqtable`), and a
collection moves them, so each table is stamped with the heap's count of
collections when its keys were last hashed; an operation that finds the
count moved rehashes first, relinking the entries it has, which allocates
nothing. This is Larceny's design with one stamp in place of its tablets
for old and young keys. At run time a table is a bloblet of kind
`eqtable`, which prints only its count.

Not yet: a table keyed by bloblets (`bloblet-identity` would be a kernel
form, for the reason above), and tables in the evaluator.

## Messages: the first line stands alone (2026-09-30)

A checker's or reader's message may run to several lines, but its first
line must be usable by itself: the REPL checks each form as it is typed
and shows only the first line, in one row, under the form (the whole
message is printed when the form is entered). So the first line says
what is wrong, and for a mismatch what was found and what was expected;
it is never a header such as "in this definition:". Each later line adds
detail and starts with two spaces, as an effect mismatch's "beyond what
is expected, it has …" does. Both checkers say the same words, so the
rule holds for both; `tests/checker.rs`, `messages_have_a_usable_first_line`,
holds every refusal in the test programs to it.

Two variables of one name in a mismatch are told apart (2026-10-10;
`Checker::show_apart`, `k-show-apart`): the first free one read keeps
its name, the next is primed, and a later line says so. A `poly`'s
binders, a `dlambda`'s, and a `moduleof`'s abstract types are bound
where they are printed and are left alone.

```
a (subr pure (a) a) is expected here, and this is a (subr pure (a') a')
  `a` and `a'` are different variables, each named `a`
```

The printers print each variable as a mark of it while the two types are
shown, and the marks are named after, in the order the text is read, so
the two checkers name them alike whatever order they print in.

While a form is typed, the line under it also says what goes next where
the cursor is (`Checker::describe_hole`): the hole is put in the whole
form, closed off, and checked with the hole read as a reserved name. An
expression there says the type it must have; an arm of a `tagcase`, the
tags no arm has taken yet; `(sum ` the expected sum's tags; elsewhere,
the shape of the innermost form around the hole, from the parser's own
refusal (``next: `(if test then else)` ``). Hole mode exists only in the
Rust checker and only while the REPL asks: no program can write a hole,
so the two checkers' agreement is untouched.

## Modules (2026-10-01, stages M1 to M5, and M7)

First-class modules, Sheldon's way as FX-91 built it
(`docs/research/first-class-modules.md`): beside globals and the REPL, not
instead of them. A module is a value; its type is a `moduleof`:

```scheme
(define counter
  (module
    (define-generative t int)                 ; abstract: up-t, down-t inside only
    (define zero t (up-t 0))
    (define inc (subr pure (t) t) (lambda (c) (up-t (+ (down-t c) 1))))
    (define value (subr pure (t) int) (lambda (c) (down-t c)))))
;; counter : (moduleof (abs t type) (val zero t) (val inc …) (val value …))
(with counter (value (inc zero)))             ; => 1
```

A module holds `define-generative` (an abstract type, whose conversions
stay inside), `define-type` (a transparent one: outside, what it is
defined as), `define-effect` (a transparent effect, `(desc e E)` in the
module's type, `(select m e)` outside), `define-datatype` (expanded as a
program's, its type and constructors the module's), `define`, `define*`
and `define-rec`; its types each see those before
them, and its values see each other, as a `letrec*`'s, made in the order
written. A typed lambda may name any item, earlier or later, so
procedures calling each other need no `define-rec`; any other value is
made when its item is, so what it names, and what the lambdas it names
name, must be made before it, or the module is refused (`` `y` uses
`get-x`, which uses `x`, defined after `y` ``); nothing is reordered. Its
definitions are never redefined. An item may re-export another module's
value under the same name, `(define x (with m x))`, the `x` in the `with`
being `m`'s, when `m` is an earlier item whose value is a module as written
(a `module` or a `load-module`, under any `plambda`, `proj`, `lambda` of no
parameters or call of none); such an item, with no type written and naming
no item but earlier such ones, is checked before the typed lambdas are
bound, so that their types may select from it (`(define-type t (select m
t))`, 2026-10-08: `modules/load-reexport.fx`, `modules/reexport.fx`).
`(with m body …)` puts `m`'s values in scope, and its types and effects
(2026-10-10, FX-91 §2.3.19): in the body, a type or effect name `m` gives
is `m`'s, whatever it is outside (`with` shadows), the innermost `with`'s
first, and one bound inside the body is that binding's; a name `m` does
not give is what it is outside (`modules/with-shadows.fx`,
`with-falls-back.fx`), or `` `n` is not a type, nor one a module opened by
`with` gives `` (`with-type-missing.fx`, `with-effect-missing.fx`). The
names are read before `m`'s type is known, so each is `(select m n)`
falling back to what it was outside, settled where the type is checked.
A description function applied, `(f d …)`, is shadowed alike: `m`'s `f`
if it gives one, else what `(f d …)` is outside, each application its
own (`with-shadows-applied.fx`, `with-applied-missing.fx`). The type
forms (`listof`, `subr`, …), the base types and `void`, `nil`, `false`,
`nat` are not shadowed, as no
definition shadows them. `(select m t)` names its type `t` in
annotations. A variable bound
to a module has its abstract types named for itself, `m..t`, so a
`let`-bound module is opaque: `(let ((m counter)) …)` makes `m..t`, not
`counter..t` (Sheldon, LFP90 §2.1.4), and nothing may leave `m`'s scope
mentioning `m..t`. A module fits a module type that has fewer of its
values, or the same in another order, or abstract types where it has
transparent ones (M4): where it is given, it is made into a module of
that type (a product of those values, by position), so `the` seals one,
`(the (moduleof (abs t type) (val zero t)) counter)` having no `inc`.
Inside other types (a list of modules, a procedure's parameter) only what
needs nothing done fits: types fewer or made abstract, the values the
same in order. A procedure's parameter that is a module may be named in
its type, `(subr pure ((o : ordered) (select o t)) (select o t))`, and the
types after it may select from it (M5, Sheldon's dependent subroutines,
of which ML's functors are a restricted form); the type says `(select $1
t)` for the first parameter's `t`. A call puts in the types of the module
its argument names, so that argument must be a module's name (bind it
with `let` first); a lambda binds its parameters in order, each seeing
those before it. At run time a module is a product of its values, in
order, and `with` extracts them. A file is a module (M7): `(load-module
"file")` reads the file's forms as a module's items and gives the module;
its type, which the REPL shows, is the file's interface. It sees only the
standard environment, not the program's globals or type names, so a file
means the same wherever it is read. A relative path is from the directory
of the file the load is written in: a loaded file's own, a program file's
(`fixpt eval`, `fixpt run`, `,load`), or the current one for a form typed
at the REPL (2026-10-27; `fixpt run` had used the current one); an error in
the file says where in it. A file whose first form is `(module-parameters
((name kind) …))` takes those as a `plambda` takes its binders (2026-10-08):
its `load-module` is `(plambda ((name kind) …) (lambda () (module item
…)))`, so that whoever loads it gives them (`((proj (load-module "f") @r))`),
and each call makes the module again, its state new. A module that has
state allocates when it is made, so it cannot be a `plambda`'s body, which
must be pure; the `lambda` of no parameters is where that happens.

Every load of a path is one value, made once (2026-10-08, `TODO.md` §68):
the first defines a hidden global, `%shared:` and the path, as the file
loaded, before the form that loads it; each load is that global, which a
loaded file may see, and which `fixpt check` does not list. So a file is checked and compiled once, however many
files load it, and making it must be pure but for reading globals: a file
whose module has state is refused, and gives instead a `lambda` that makes
it, which each client applies. And so are its abstract types
(2026-10-27, `docs/research/derived-signatures.md`, Q2): a load's are
transparent descriptions of the hidden global's own, named for the file
(`x.fx..t`), so every binding of a load of a file, in any file, has the
same ones, and a file may offer an operation on another's abstract type to
whoever loads that one too (`modules/load-shared.fx`). Sheldon's rule for
two textually equal `input`s, which here is true by construction. A
maker's modules, made by applying it, keep types of their own, named by
the variable each is bound to. `(load-input "file")` reads a file of one expression and gives its
value, as Sheldon's `input` does: the evaluator's files are each a
`lambda` from the modules it is given to the module it makes, applied by
the conductor (`conductor.fx`). Such a
file sees its parameters as well as the standard environment
(`modules/load-parameters.fx`, through `module-files/tally-twice.fx`). A
path `fx26:name` names a module file built in: the front end's reader and
parser (`eager-reader.fx`, `parser.fx`, `parser-exps.fx`, `parser-top.fx`),
each a file of the reader's regions that loads the one before; the front
end makes the last at its own (`reader.fx`). Two are for code generators
(`TODO.md` §97): `(load-input "fx26:arm64.fx")`, the arm64 encoder, and
`(load-input "fx26:convention.fx")`, a native convention's declaration
read as data, a `convention` (its types in `fx26:convention-types.fx`;
`docs/research/declared-conventions.md`, step 6). A file that loads itself, or a file
that loads it, is refused, the chain named (`` a load cycle: `a.fx` loads
`b.fx` loads `a.fx` ``, 2026-10-10).

A file's module type is a type (2026-10-27, Heist's request,
`docs/research/derived-signatures.md`): `(load-moduleof "file" arg …)` is
the type of the module a `load-module` of the file makes given `arg …`,
and `(load-input-moduleof "file" arg …)` of the module a `load-input`
file's value makes. The file is loaded as a load of it is, once, and
nothing is made of it here. Its value's type is taken apart by the
arguments: a `poly` takes as many descriptions as it binds, as `proj`
does; a procedure of no parameters is applied, as a file of parameters'
maker is; a procedure's parameters take one argument each, a module's name
where the types after it select from it, as a call does. What is left must
be a module's type (`` what `f.fx` makes, given these, is a …, not a module
``). So a file that is a function of the modules it uses types each by the
file that makes it, with no signature written out
(`modules/load-moduleof.fx`):

```scheme
;; made-buffer.fx, a load-input file: a function of a scheduler
(plambda ((ed region) (world effect))
  (lambda ((core (load-moduleof "made-core.fx" ed world)))
    (module …)))
;; made-client.fx: of a scheduler, and the buffers made over that one
(plambda ((ed region) (world effect))
  (lambda ((core (load-moduleof "made-core.fx" ed world))
           (buffer (load-input-moduleof "made-buffer.fx" ed world core)))
    (module …)))
```

The type's abstract types are its own, bound by what has the type: a
parameter of it stands for its argument's, so one type fits every module
the file makes, and two such modules' types stay apart

## Higher kinds (2026-10-04)

A binder, a type family's parameter, a generative type's parameter, and a
module's abstract component may be a *description function*, of an arrow
kind `(=> (k1 … kn) k)`: FX-91's `dlambda`, with regions, places, sizes and
conventions among what it may take, and an effect among what it may give,
which FX-91 had no use for. Test programs: `tests/programs/higher-kinds/`;
the design and its sources: `docs/research/higher-kinds.md`.

```scheme
(define-type stack-sig
  (moduleof (abs stack (=> (type) type))
            (val empty (poly ((a type)) (stack a)))
            (val push (poly ((a type)) (subr (alloc @heap) (a (stack a)) (stack a))))
            …))
(define list-stack stack-sig
  (module (define-generative (stack (a type)) (listof a @heap)) …))
```

- **Functions.** A function is a variable of an arrow kind, a
  `(dlambda ((x k) …) d)`, a `define-type` of one, a type family or
  generative type with parameters named and not applied, a type form's
  name (`listof` is `(=> (type region) type)`), or `(select m f)`.
- **Applied**, `(f d …)`: a `dlambda` reduces (beta); one that only
  applies a function to its parameters in order is that function (eta);
  a variable applied stays an application, equal only to one of the same
  function to equal descriptions (FX-91's rule). Arity and kinds are
  checked where the function is known, and again where a `select` says
  what it is.
- **Inferred first-order only.** An application against an application
  finds the function; against a generative type applied, that generative
  type. Anything else, `(f a)` against `(productof …)` say, says to use
  `proj`: no higher-order unification (Jones's restriction, and FX-91's).
- **Effect families.** `(define-type rw (dlambda ((r region)) (maxeff
  (read r) (write r))))` is applied inside an effect, `(subr (rw r) …)`;
  a variable of kind `(=> (region) effect)` applied is an atom of its own,
  treated as an effect variable is: by masking, by the licence, and by
  effect summaries. A function to an effect takes no types.
- **Modules.** `(abs f (=> (type) type))` is an abstract type constructor,
  or `(abs (f (a type)) type)`, its parameters named as
  `define-generative`'s are (2026-10-10; a list of names alone, `(abs (a
  b) type)`, is several abstract types); a module's `(define-generative (f
  (a type)) rep)` makes one, its `up-f`/`down-f` polymorphic in its
  parameters. A module type with one kind of constructor is no subtype of
  one with another.
- **Soundness.** What a function is given counts as invariant; recursion
  through applications alone is refused (`(define-type (fix (f (=> (type)
  type))) (f (fix f)))`: with `f` the identity, no type); an abstract
  constructor's representation is unseen, so what it is given is kept,
  cautiously, everywhere a knot through the store could be tied; and
  `letfreeze`, calls that may not end, and `finite` sizes look through an
  application at what it was given.

### Abstract, generative, parametric: three things, not one

Three properties, independent of each other, often run together:

1. **Kind: plain or parametric.** A type, or a type constructor of kind
   `(=> (type) type)`. This says nothing about hiding or identity: the
   transparent `(dlambda ((a type)) (listof a @heap))` is higher-kinded
   and hides nothing.
2. **Abstract: hidden from whoever looks.** Abstraction depends on the
   viewer. Outside `(moduleof (abs t type) …)`, `t` is only a variable;
   inside the module it is whatever was defined. A `poly`-bound type
   variable is abstract the same way to the body it is given to. This is
   the existential view (Mitchell and Plotkin): abstraction is a matter of
   scope and interface.
3. **Generative: a type with an identity of its own.** `define-generative`
   makes a type distinct from its representation everywhere, even where
   the representation is in plain view; only `up-t` and `down-t` cross.
   This is a property of the definition, not of the viewer: a top-level
   `define-generative` is generative and not abstract, since anyone may
   name it and use its conversions.

Every combination exists, each plain or parametric:

|                | transparent                     | abstract                                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| not generative | `define-type`, `dlambda`        | `(abs t …)` sealed over a `define-type`                 |
| generative     | a top-level `define-generative` | a module's `define-generative`, conversions kept inside |

Each can be had from the other, and need not be:

- **Abstraction from generativity:** a generative type whose conversions
  are kept private cannot be related to anything outside, so it is
  abstract in effect. A module's `define-generative` is this:
  `up-stack` and `down-stack` stay inside.
- **Generativity from abstraction:** seal a transparent definition behind
  an `abs`, and give each binding of the module a fresh name for it; two
  bindings then have different abstract types. Binding a module does this
  (its abstract types renamed `m..t`, fresh each time), as ML has it by
  sealing and functors (`tests/programs/modules/transparent-as-abstract.fx`,
  and `higher-kinds/container-functor.fx` for a constructor).

Neither reduces to the other. A generative type stays distinct where all
is visible (two `define-generative`s of the same representation), which
sealing cannot give inside the scope that sees through it. Abstraction can
hide what is not new at all: inside `container-functor.fx`'s module, `f`
*is* `listof … @heap`, and only outsiders are kept from knowing.

The difference shows in inference: an unknown function is found from a
*generative* type applied (`(stack int)` against `(f int)` gives `f =
stack`), since a generative type is a fixed name; never from a transparent
one, which is why `higher-kinds/cannot-infer.fx` asks for `proj`.

## Variadic procedures (2026-09-29)

FX-87's, which FX-91 dropped (the user's request, 2026-09-29):
`(vsubr E T R)` is the type of a procedure that takes any number of
arguments, each a `T`, with latent effect `E`, giving an `R`.
`(vlambda xs body …)` makes one, `xs` bound to the list of the arguments,
a `(listof T acyclic)`; `(vlambda (xs T) body …)` says `T`. A call is
written as any call, `(f a b c)`; `(apply f xs)` calls one on the elements
of a list:

```
(define add-up (vsubr (maxeff spin (read (globals total))) int int) (vlambda xs (total xs)))
(add-up 1 2 3)                                          ; 6
(apply add-up (the (listof int @heap) (cons 5 (cons 6 nil))))  ; 11
```

| name       | type                                                                                                         |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `%vlambda` | `(poly ((e effect) (t type) (r type)) (subr pure ((subr e ((listof t acyclic)) r)) (vsubr e t r)))`          |
| `apply`    | `(poly ((e effect) (t type) (r type) (g region)) (subr (maxeff e (read g)) ((vsubr e t r) (listof t g)) r))` |

`vsubr` is a generative type, the first in both checkers, declared `(vsubr
(e effect +) (t type -) (r type +))` over `(subr e ((listof t acyclic))
r)`, with no `up-` or `down-`: nothing sees inside it. `vlambda` is read as
`(%vlambda (lambda ((xs (listof T acyclic))) body …))`. At run time every
call passes its count, as Larceny's MacScheme machine does, so that
`apply` can spread a list (the user's choice, over the caller making the
list). The lowered program runs Scheme's own `(lambda xs …)` and `apply`.
On the machines, `%vlambda` makes a cellular closure over the procedure of
the word `rest; free 0; ttailcall 1`, where the routine `rest` lists the
frame's values, as many as the call gave (on the cellular machines the
count is the frame's depth). That word has a register twin,

    vargs; save 0; cellular rest 0; setreg 1; lexical 0; pop 0; tailinvoke 1

which native code runs as machine code. Every native call puts its count
in `x9`, Larceny's way. `vargs` must be the first instruction, in place of
`args n`, and it keeps the count and the argument registers. `rest` lists
them, past 8 unpacking the eighth, which holds a list of the rest. The
procedure of one list is then called as a tail call. A foreign call into
native code passes the count in `x9` as well.

`(apply f xs)` compiles to `swap; field 3; tcall 1` in stack code: that
procedure, called on `xs`. Both register compilers, the Rust one and the
one in FX-26, compile it as `f` and `xs` in order. Then `f`'s free value 0
goes to a frame slot, `xs` to REG1, and `invoke 1` runs, or `tailinvoke 1`
in tail position. A variadic call costs about 4% more natively than making
the same list with `cons` (a million calls of 10 arguments). The test is
`programs/native/variadic.fx`: global and run-time `vlambda`s, 0 to 11
arguments, and `apply`, in and out of tail position, collecting often.

**`apply` gives a fresh list** (2026-09-29, F11 of
`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`). A `vlambda`'s list is typed
`acyclic`, so it must be one nothing else can write. `apply` therefore
copies the list it is given, as Scheme's does (R7RS 4.1.4: "a newly
allocated list"; Larceny and Racket both copy). A cyclic list is an error,
as Racket makes it, not a loop, since `apply` says no `spin`. The copy is
skipped where the checker found the list at `acyclic`: both checkers note
such an `apply` (`apply_shares`, and the fact -500 in FX-26's), and every
compiler reads it.

**`list`** (2026-09-29) is standard. Its type is `(poly ((t type) (r region))
(vsubr (alloc r) t (listof t r)))`: `(list 1 2 3)` is a fresh list at any
region, as a `cons` chain may be, so `(define xs (listof int @heap) (list 1 2
3))` makes a list that can be written, and at `acyclic` the allocation is
pure. The region comes from the expected type, as a `cons` chain's does. As
a value it is a `vsubr`, so `(apply list xs)` copies `xs`. (Made region
polymorphic the same day, the user's choice; it had given `acyclic` only.)
It runs as follows:

- Lowered, it is Scheme's `list`.
- On the stack machines, it is the runtime primitive `list`.
- In register code, both register compilers put the pairs in line: each
  argument that is not simple, or is in a register, is made and kept
  first, in order, then the pairs are made from the last onto `nil`.
  The native compiler makes those pairs inline, so a `list` costs what
  the same `cons` chain does.
- As a value, it is `%vlambda`'s closure over a procedure that copies its
  one list, as `datum-list` does: `apply` gives a list at `acyclic` as it
  is (F11), and `list` may give it at a region that can be written.

A substitution drops a read, allocation or await at data frozen into the
heap, as `Checker::frozen` does for an expression's effect, so that
`list` at `acyclic` is pure. Two checker changes came with it, in both
checkers. A polymorphic `vsubr`
is called as a subroutine of as many parameters as there are arguments,
each of its element type. And a polymorphic variable given as an argument
to a polymorphic procedure is instantiated at that parameter, which then
solves more: in `(apply list xs)`, `apply`'s effect.

**`append` and `string-append`** (the user's, 2026-10-13) are standard
`vsubr`s too, called with any count of arguments, as Scheme's are:

| name            | type                                                                                                  |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `append`        | `(poly ((r region)) (poly ((t type)) (vsubr (maxeff (read r) (alloc r)) (listof t r) (listof t r))))` |
| `string-append` | `(vsubr pure string string)`                                                                          |

`append` copies every list but the last, which it shares, so all of them,
and the result, are at one region (it had been two lists, the first at a
region of its own). `(append)` is `nil`, `(string-append)` is `""`. A call
runs as the runtime's primitive of any count, in every machine; as a value
each is `%vlambda`'s closure, as `list` is, over a procedure applying the
operation to its list of arguments (`%fx26-append-list`,
`%fx26-string-append-list`; `Compiler::vsubr_list_op`, `c-vsubr-list-op`).
They are built in, as `list` is, not definitions in FX-26: there is no
prelude every program sees (`TODO.md` §93), and a call of a definition would
make the list of its arguments, where the primitive takes them as they are.
Test: `programs/native/variadic-standard.fx`, on every machine.

## Immutable data, arrays and symbols (M12 step 7, 2026-09-26)

Taken from FX-91 where it had them. Products and sums are immutable, so they
are in no region and making one is pure; at run time each is a frozen
bloblet, a product's fields in order and a sum's tag (a symbol) then its
value.

| form                                    | type                                         | notes                                       |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| `(product (l e) …)`                     | `(productof (l T) …)`                        | covariant; labels in order                  |
| `(extract e l)`                         | `T` of label `l`                             | pure                                        |
| `(sum tag e)`                           | `(sumof (tag T))`                            | fits any sum with that tag: width subtyping |
| `(tagcase e (tag x body) … (else y b))` | the arms' common type                        | `else` sees only the tags not named         |
| `(define-datatype t (tag T …) …)`       | a sum of products, and a constructor per tag | FX-91's; expanded as it is read             |

A `tagcase` arm may take a product apart, `(tag (x y) body)`, which is how a
`define-datatype` variant's members are named. Without an `else`, the arms
must cover every tag.

**Generative types** (2026-09-27; `docs/research/generative-types.md`,
`docs/research/gadts.md` N1–N2). `(define-generative name rep)`, or with
parameters `(define-generative (name (param kind [+|-]) …) rep)`, makes a
new type, equal only to itself, and two conversions, `up-name : rep →
name` and `down-name : name → rep`, the identity at run time:

```
(define-generative ty-id int)
(define-generative (tree (a type)) (sumof (leaf a) (node (productof (l (tree a)) (r (tree a))))))
```

- **Opaque to comparison.** `(name d …)` is compared with `(name d′ …)`
  argument by argument, never unfolded; with anything else, never equal. A
  parameter is invariant unless marked `+` or `-`, which `rep` must bear
  out (checked once); a region or place parameter is always invariant.
- **Transparent to safety.** The analyses of what a value holds look
  through the name: regions (masking, escape from a `letregion`),
  `writes_in` (`letfreeze`), the store-knot rule and self-application for
  `spin`; for what the name was given, cautiously. Size-change sees the
  conversions as the identity, so a walk of a `(tree a)` through
  `down-tree` ends.
- **Only its conversions see inside it**, which is all hiding is for now.
- **Recursion through the name is never expanded**, so `rep` may be
  non-regular: `(nest a)` may mention `(nest (productof (l a) (r a)))`.
- Expanded as it is read into the checker-only form and two ordinary
  definitions, so nothing downstream changes.

**Lemmas: proofs as erased identity coercions** (2026-09-27;
`src/lemma.rs`, `docs/research/gadts.md` N3). A definition whose declared
type is a proposition is a lemma, once its body proves it:

```
(define tree-up (proves (poly ((a type) (b type)) (<= (tree a) (tree b)) (<= a b)))
  (lambda (f t)
    (up-tree (tagcase (down-tree t)
               (leaf x (sum leaf (f x)))
               (node (l r) (sum node (product (l (tree-up f l)) (r (tree-up f r)))))))))
```

- `(proves (<= A B))`, or `(proves (poly (binder …) (<= A B) (<= X Y) …))`,
  is the type of a function from a coercion per hypothesis and an `A` to a
  `B`, with effect `spin`: a proof need not end, and is never run unless
  called on purpose (to debug it, say).
- **The body must be a guarded structural identity**: it takes apart only
  what it was given, rebuilds the same tags and labels, in order, applies
  a hypothesis or a proof only to what was given at the same place, and
  uses itself only under a constructor it has rebuilt. A coercion it hands
  a proof is a hypothesis, a proof, itself (guarded), or a lambda whose
  parameter's type is written and whose body rebuilds it. Where it fails,
  the error says `this does not prove `A ≤ B`: …` and why.
- **Its existence is the proof.** Subtyping uses a lemma where its rules
  alone fail: the trail restored first, then the lemma's hypotheses,
  instantiated to fit, compared assuming what is being shown. So an
  invariant generative type widens, a non-regular one (`nest`) too, and one
  generative type stands for another of the same shape, in the direction
  proved only (`tests/programs/lemmas/`).

**The `data` kind, and `acyclic`** (2026-09-27; `docs/research/gadts.md`
N6, `docs/research/confirmation.md` CF0). A binder `(t data)` takes only
data: types built from base types, `datum`, products and sums, and pairs
and bloblets that are frozen (`const` or `acyclic`), of data. No procedure,
no storage that can be written, no generative type (reading one in would
be `up`). Every data type is a type; the checker works out whether a type
is data, so only binders say `data`.

A `data` binder is data at a place (2026-09-30, F13;
`docs/research/shapes.md`): `(t data p)` takes data whose frozen parts are
in the heap or in place `p`, bound before it, as `(r region p)` is; plain
`(t data)` takes data in the heap only. What walks data reads it, so
`acyclic?` and `length-is?` are `(poly ((p place) (t data p)) (subr (read
(const p)) (t) bool))` (and `(l int)` for `length-is?`): `pure` on heap data,
and naming the place on data frozen into one, so that a closure walking it
cannot outlive it. `p` is found from the argument's type.

`(acyclic e (x body) else)` runs `body` with `x` bound to `e`'s value at
`acyclic` where it was `const`, if that value has no cycle, and `else` if it
has one. It is sugar for

```
(let ((%acyclic-value e))
  (if (acyclic? %acyclic-value)
      (let ((x (certify-acyclic %acyclic-value))) body)
      else))
```

- `acyclic?`, of type `(poly ((t data)) (subr pure (t) bool))`, walks the
  value's pairs, vectors and bloblets, marking the path it is on, so
  sharing is fine and only a way back to the path is a cycle.
- `certify-acyclic` is accepted only on a variable, in the branch where
  `acyclic?` has just found that same binding acyclic (by binding, not
  name, so shadowing cannot fool it), as `tagcase` refines. Frozen data
  cannot be written again, so the finding stays true.

So data frozen with `set-cdr!` inside a `letfreeze` (`const`) can be
certified `acyclic`, and walked by a procedure that needs no `spin`
(`tests/programs/datum/acyclic.fx`). A `read` that makes cycles from datum
labels is still to come.

**Datums** (2026-10-08, `TODO.md` §51). `datum`, what a reader reads, is a
union (`check::DATUM`, defined in both checkers' standard environment):

    (union int f64 char bool string symbol nil
           (pairof datum datum acyclic) vector bytevector)

Its pairs are frozen and acyclic: covariant, so a `(listof datum acyclic)`
or a `(pairof int nil acyclic)` is a datum, and walked with `car` and
`cdr` with no `spin`. `cons` and `list` make one; `typecase` and the shape
predicates take it apart. Frozen data that may have a cycle, at `const`,
is no datum until `acyclic` finds it has none: the region is where a
reader of datum labels (`#1=(a . #1#)`) would say what it made, a datum's
cyclic counterpart being the same union at `const`.

`car` and `cdr` take apart a union with a pair member, as they take apart
a pair that may be `nil`: every machine checks the argument is a pair and
traps if not, so Scheme code that takes apart data it has not tested
ports as written. The `datum-` constructors, predicates and accessors are
gone (2026-10-08): `cons`, `pair?`, `symbol?`, `car`, and narrowing, do
what they did; the casts among them (`datum-int-value` and kin) did no
check at run time, and a `typecase` or a test now does. What remains is
what nothing else does: `datum-list`, a copy of a list at any region;
`datum-dotted`; `datum-list->vector` and `datum-list->bytevector`; and
the tests `datum-int?` (a fixnum, narrowing to `int`), `datum-byte?`,
`datum-proper-list?`.

**`quote` and `quasiquote`** (2026-10-08). Any datum may be quoted, not
only a symbol: both parsers rewrite `'(a 1 "s" (b . c) #(x))` into what
builds it (`Checker::quoted`, `quoted` in `parser.fx`): an atom is
itself, a symbol `(quote s)`, `()` is `nil`, a list or dotted list
`cons`es, a vector `datum-list->vector` of its list, the whole a
`(the datum …)`. Under `` ` ``, `,e` at depth 1 is `e`, and `,@e` is `e`
(a `(listof datum R)`) appended to what follows; a nested `` ` `` deepens
and `,` shallows, as R7RS counts. What it builds with, `cons`, `append`,
`datum-list->vector` and the `nil` that ends a list, is the standard one,
through `(with #%fx …)`, whatever the program binds those names to; a
bare `'()` is the plain `nil`, an empty list of any type. Each `with` made
is at a place of its own (an item's, the closing parenthesis, the `#(`):
the checker written in FX-26 keeps its facts by place, and one `with`'s
fact read as another's once made native code call a program's own `cons`.
A quoted datum is **interned** (2026-10-08): the one object equal to it,
as a symbol is. The rewrite marks what a quote builds `(%quote …)`, and
`%quote` is the heap's hash-consing (`Heap::intern_datum`, `heap/intern.rs`):
a pair found by its interned car and cdr, a string, float or vector by its
contents. Each compiler makes a quote of literals and symbols once, at
compile time, interned; the lowering writes a Scheme constant, interned
(`%fx26-intern-datum`); a quote of a string, float or vector is interned
as it is evaluated. So a quote is one object each time, at every copy
inlining makes of it, and equal quotes are one object: `(eq? '(1 2) '(1
2))` is `#t` on every machine (R7RS leaves it unspecified). Under `` ` ``,
each part no `,` reaches, a subtree or the items after the last `,`, is
such a quote: `` `(,x (a b) c) ``'s `((a b) c)` is `'((a b) c)`. The table
is in the heap, the global `%fx26-interned-data`, so an image keeps it;
its entries are weak pairs (`docs/research/weak-references.md`), so what
only the table holds is collected; hashed by address, it is relinked by the
first intern after a collection, as an `eqtable` is. The
evaluator written in FX-26, whose values are its own, interns them in a
table of its own (`ev-intern`), pairs by their interned parts and strings
by their characters, so it says what the machines say.
A `,@` before a dotted tail (`` `(,@xs . y) ``) is refused: `append` takes
a list.
A quoted bytevector is refused, for now: FX-26 cannot read a bytevector's
bytes, so its parser could not build one.

**Distinct regions** (a prototype, 2026-10-11;
`docs/research/region-anti-aliasing.md`, variant (C)). FX-26 has no
anti-aliasing rule: two region binders may be given one region. A region
binder marked `distinct` last, `(poly ((r1 region distinct) (r2 region))
…)`, asks for Lucassen's rule (MIT/LCS/TR-408 §3.6.3) for itself alone:
each instantiation must give it a region known not to overlap the other
binders' nor any region the `poly`'s type mentions (different constants,
the heap, a fresh region, a `letregion`'s, or a `distinct` binder of an
enclosing procedure); else it is refused ("`r1` is distinct, and is given
r, as `r2` is"). Inside, a `distinct` binder aliases nothing else in
scope, so a write to another region keeps the facts a test found through
it (`kill_paths`); a `letregion`'s region is distinct too. A `poly` whose
binder is `distinct` is a subtype only of one whose is. Not yet: in
`module-parameters`, in modules' signatures, and anything a transpiler to
Rust's `&mut` would build on it (the note's §9). Tests:
`regions/distinct-*.fx`.

**Sizes** (2026-09-27; `docs/research/sizes.md`, N5a). `(nlist T size)` is
a list frozen `acyclic` with `size` elements, or `(nlist T size p)` frozen
into place `p`; a size is a natural number or `finite`, some number not
known. `(nlist T finite)` and `(listof T acyclic)` are each other's subtypes;
a `nlist` is covariant and forgets its size to `finite`, and a call's
size binder is solved through it: `(length l)` of a `(listof T acyclic)`
is a `(nat finite)`, shown `nat` (kayak's report, 2026-10-11). A list at
a region, which may be written, is no `nlist`: `length` of one is refused,
and the message names `list-length` (any list, `nat`, reading its region).

- `nil` is a `(nlist T 0)`; `cons` onto a `nlist` makes one one longer (where
  a `nlist` is expected, or onto a variable of `nlist` type); `car`, `cdr` and
  `null?` work on a `nlist` as on any list, the tail a `nlist` one shorter.
- Size-change descends a `nlist`, so a walk of one needs no `spin`.
- `(confirm-length e k (x body) else)` runs `body` with `x : (nlist T k)`
  if `e`, a frozen list, is proper and has `k` elements, and `else`
  otherwise: sugar over `length-is?` and `certify-length`, the latter
  accepted only on the binding just confirmed with that length.
- **Size variables** (N5b): a binder `(n size)`; sizes `n`, `(+ n m)`,
  `(- n 1)`; an unsolved size binder is `finite`. In the branches of
  `(null? xs)`, `xs : (nlist T n)`, the checker knows `n = 0`, or `n - 1 ≥
  0`; `nil`, `cons`, a `nlist`'s tail and comparing sizes use what it knows,
  and every size is a natural. So `map` keeps a length and `append` adds
  two (`tests/programs/sizes/`). A size binder may be `finite` (given, or
  solved, or left unsolved) only where it is the size of at most one
  parameter's own `(nlist T n)` or `(nat n)`, and of nothing else a caller
  supplies or can write: that one argument has some size, which `n` then
  stands for, and everywhere else `finite` only forgets it
  (`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`, F4).
- **Naturals** (N5d): `(nat s)` is a natural that is exactly the size `s`,
  and `nat` is `(nat finite)`, some natural; every `nat` is an `int`. A
  natural literal checks as either. `(+ a b)` of naturals is a natural of
  the sum; `(- a b)` is a natural where the facts show `a - b ≥ 0`, and an
  `int` otherwise. The operands that count are literals, variables, and
  `+`, `-`, `length`, `string-length` and `array-length`; any other is only
  told it is an `int`. A variable
  bound to a `nat` of no known size, a parameter or a `let`, is given a
  size of its own, named after it, and forgotten, as `finite`, outside its
  scope. Comparisons of naturals (`<`, `<=`, `>`, `>=`, `=`) teach facts in
  their branches, and so does `(= n 0)` in its `else`: `n ≥ 1`. The branches
  of an `if` of naturals of sizes not shown equal join at `nat`. `length`
  takes a `(nlist T n)` to a `(nat n)`, and `confirm-length` takes a
  variable holding a `(nat s)` for its length, as well as a literal.
  Size-change counts a `nat` parameter down with no test bounding it: every
  argument passed for it is checked a natural.
  `string-length` and `array-length` are naturals too, and an array's
  length, which never changes, bounds a count up as a string's does.
- `(narrayof T n R)` is an array of `n` elements (`TODO.md` §77, N5c);
  `(arrayof T R)` is one of length `finite`, which any array is (its
  length may be forgotten, as a `nlist`'s; its elements stay invariant).
  A `make-array` (`rmake-array`) of a natural of size `n` is a
  `(narrayof T n R)`, kept through `(the (arrayof T R) …)`;
  `array-length` takes a `(narrayof T n R)` to a `(nat n)`; and a variable
  bound to an array of a length not known is given one of its own, as a
  `nat` is (forgotten where its scope ends, by F8's rule). An
  `array-ref` or `array-set!` of a variable's array whose index the facts
  show below its length is recorded (`NodeFacts::in_bounds`), and §86's
  debts count it discharged; the compilers still check it. Of the ports'
  732 array indexes, 34 are so shown (2026-10-12): most count with an
  `int`, not a `nat`, down from the end, or into an array of arrays.
  `(confirm-nat e (n body) else)` runs `body` with `n : nat` if the integer
  `e` is no less than 0, and `else` otherwise: sugar over `nat?` and
  `certify-nat`, the latter accepted only on the binding `nat?` has just
  confirmed, as `acyclic`'s is.

**Parametric datatypes** (2026-09-27). `(define-datatype (name (param kind)
…) (tag type …) …)` defines a type family, which its variants may mention
with the same parameters, and constructors polymorphic in them:

```
(define-datatype (rose (t type) (r region))
  (leaf t)
  (node (listof (rose t r) r)))
```

`(rose int acyclic)` is a finite tree of ints; `leaf` is `(poly ((t type)
(r region)) (subr pure (t) (rose t r)))`. A type family mentioning itself
with the same descriptions, `(rose t r)` inside `rose`, is the type being
made: a knot, as `dletrec` ties. One whose descriptions change as it
recurses (`(grow (listof t @r))` inside `grow`) would never end, and is
refused.

Arrays and symbols need no rules of their own: `(arrayof T R)` is a type,
and `make-array`, `array-ref`, `array-set!` and `array-length` are ordinary
polymorphic constants, as `cons` is; `symbol` is a base type, `'name` a
literal, with `string->symbol`, `symbol->string`, `symbol=?`, and
`string-hash`.

**Tables** are written in FX-26 (`src/table.fx`, `fixpt_fx26::TABLE`):
generic in key, value and region, a bloblet of the key's hash and
equality, an array of association-list buckets, and a count; the buckets
double past one entry each. `make-table`, `table-ref`, `table-set!`,
`table-has?`, `table-count`, and `symbol-hash`.

Writing them asked for two things of the checker, both FX-91's in spirit:

- **Type abbreviations with parameters**, `(define-type (table (k type)
  (v type) (r region)) …)`, expanded by substitution where used. Sugar:
  nothing downstream sees them. (Since 2026-09-27 a family may mention
  itself with the same descriptions: see "Parametric datatypes".)
- **A `plambda` checked against a `poly`** checks its body against the
  `poly`'s body, binders renamed, so a signature's types reach inside a
  polymorphic definition: `nil` in a branch knows which list it is.

A list with nothing to say what it is a list of, `(cons 1 nil)` at the top
of a program, is a `(pairof int (listof int R) R)`, a list (2026-10-08,
`DONE.md` §48).

**Reading, in FX-26** (M12 step 8). The eager reader written in FX-26
builds `syn` values, a `define-datatype` of what it read with where each
piece starts and ends; each also carries its plain `datum`, made once, for
the marks and for Scheme. `crate::syn` walks them (frozen bloblets, so
nothing has to run) into the Rust checker's `Syntax`, character positions
turned into byte offsets. On every text tried, spans included, that is
exactly what the Rust reader produces, and
`Fx26Session::run_program_read_by_fx26` runs a program with no Rust reader
on the way. It is slow: the reader runs on the bytecode VM a character at
a time.

**Checking, in FX-26** (M12 step 10). `src/check.fx` is the Rust checker
(`check.rs`, `infer.rs`, and the description half of `parse.rs` and
`top.rs`) written again in FX-26, rule for rule and message for message,
over the FX-26 parser's trees. Types live in an arena of indexes, so a
recursive type is a cycle, as in Rust. Effects are sorted lists of atoms,
in an order of FX-26's own. Fresh regions are a variant no program can
write, where Rust uses uninterned symbols. A first pass reads each tree's
descriptions where the Rust parser reads them, so the first error found is
the same one. `Fx26Session::check_with_own_checker` returns, for each
definition and expression, `define name : type ! effect` or `type !
effect`, or the first error with its span. Up to the order of atoms in a
`maxeff`, that is what the Rust checker says:

- on every test program;
- on every program written into the crate's tests (80 accepted, 90
  rejected);
- on the whole front end, the checker itself included (738 forms).

The checker also records where each `extract`'s field is, which the FX-26
compiler needs, so the compiler now checks before it compiles.

**Two kinds of addition.** A form the checker needs a rule for is kernel,
and will be written twice, here and in the checker written in FX-26 (step
10): the bloblet forms, products, sums and `tagcase`. Everything else is a
constant with a type, or sugar expanded before checking (`define-datatype`),
and costs nothing there. Mutable records are left out: a bloblet is one.

**Names.** Form names are reserved, which is why a program cannot define
`sum`. Scheme would let a binding shadow a form; FX-26 parses before it
knows what is bound, so for now the rule is fewer tempting names. Modules
(FX-91's first-class ones, whose values half products already give) wait
until the checker written in FX-26 spans more than one file.

## Specifications, `assert` and `assume`, `fixpt test` and `fixpt verify` (2026-10-23)

A procedure's type may say what it requires and ensures
(`docs/research/typed-cellular-code.md`, §8):
`(subr E ((x : int) …) T (requires p) (ensures r q))`, `p` and `q`
propositions of its `int`s, `nat`s and `bool`s (linear relations, `not`,
`and`, `or`, `implies`), `r` naming the result; `(bool (then P …) (else
Q …))`, what a test proves, is an `ensures` too. The checkers carry them
and compare two by behavioural subtyping; the verifier of the cellular
code proves them (`typed_cells.rs`).

A proposition, or an integer in one, may call a *logic function*
(`docs/research/specs-and-counterexamples.md`, §5): a global procedure,
`pure`, of a `bool` result (a proposition) or an `int`'s or `nat`'s (an
integer), each argument an integer where it takes one, else a parameter
or the result, named:

```
(define insert (subr (maxeff spin (read (globals insert))) ((e : int) (xs : ints)) ints
                 (ensures r (implies (sorted? xs) (sorted? r))))
  …)
```

`pure` asks that it end: a recursive one is a local `letrec`'s, over
lists that are not written (`(listof int acyclic)`), which the checkers
show end. The verifier's prover knows a call as a function and nothing
more (equal arguments, equal values), and gives a call of one in the
cells the term a specification names it by: so `(assert (sorted? (insert
e xs)))`, after `(assume (sorted? xs))`, is proved from `insert`'s
`ensures` (`specs/logic-harness.fx`). The SMT solver is given each logic
function's definition too, where its body is of what it reads (`if`,
`null?`, `pair?`, `car` and `cdr` of a list a test showed a pair, `cons`,
arithmetic, comparisons, calls of logic functions), over integers,
booleans and finite lists of `int`s, of `bool`s or of lists so (not of
`nat`s), as `(List Int)`, `(List (List Int))` (`fixpt_fx26::smt_theory`);
so an `ensures` about what `insert` computes can be proved, where it is
inductive (`specs/logic-insert.fx`), and where it is not, the report
names the recursive call whose value the proof took wrongly:

```
… not proved, as the proof knows too little of a call. It holds for e = 1,
xs = (the ints (list 0)); but for all the proof knows, (insert 1 (cdr (the
ints (list 0)))) could be (-1), where it is (1). …
```

Whether an argument is of the type the function takes is not checked as
the specification is read; a run checking it is (`fixpt test`).

Within a body, two standard procedures say more
(`docs/research/specs-and-counterexamples.md`, §4):

| | type | at run time | to the verifier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `(assert p)` | `(subr pure ((p : bool)) unit (requires p))` | fails, "assertion failed", where `p` is false | a VC, then known |
| `(assume p)` | `(subr pure ((p : bool)) unit (ensures r p))` | fails, "assumption not met", where `p` is false | known after it |

`fixpt test INPUT [RUNS]` tests what a program says by running it, with
no prover (`fixpt_fx26::spec_tests`). The program is run with each
specified `lambda` checking its own specification as it runs; then each
procedure that does nothing another run could see, and whose parameters
are `int`s, `nat`s, `bool`s or lists of those, is called on RUNS drawn
inputs (small and edge values, then pseudo-random ones, the same each
time). An input its `requires` or an `assume` does not take is passed
over; a specification that does not hold in a run, its own or a callee's,
or an `assert` that does not, is a counterexample, shown with the input.
A *harness* is a procedure, of `unit` result, that `assert`s what it
tests, its parameters the values it is tested at:

```
(define insert-keeps-sorted
  (subr (maxeff spin (read @heap) (alloc @heap) (read (globals sorted? insert))) (int ints) unit)
  (lambda (e xs) (begin (assume (sorted? xs)) (assert (sorted? (insert e xs))))))
```

`fixpt verify INPUT [NAME …]` proves what a program says
(`fixpt_fx26::verifying`): the Rust checker checks it, the Rust compiler
compiles it with certificates, and each word, or each NAME's, is verified
against its own. A word not verified is shown with the obligations that
fail or are left open, each at its place in the source (the innermost
expression whose cells it is said of, `FILE:LINE:COLUMN` and the
expression; in the REPL, the expression); it exits 1 if any is. In the
REPL, `,verify [NAME …]` does the same for what has been defined there
(each form the checker took, and each file `,load` ran, replayed as one
program):

```
fx26> (define bad (-> ((x : int)) int (ensures r (<= 0 r)))
        (lambda (x) (if (< x 0) x (- 0 x))))
fx26> ,verify bad
; `bad`: not verified
;   at `x`: the ensures: false: with x = -1, the result is -1
;   at `(- 0 x)`: the ensures: false: with x = 1, the result is -1
```

With an SMT solver (z3, or what `FIXPT_SMT` names), each obligation the
verifier leaves open is asked of it (`fixpt_fx26::smt`), and sorted
(`docs/research/specs-and-counterexamples.md`, §3 and §8):

- **proved**, and the word verified with it (`square.fx`'s `x · x ≥ 0`);
- **false**: the solver's values of the parameters, run with the
  specifications checked, break one, said with the input;
- **not inductive**: the run keeps it, so the proof took a call to give
  what it does not; the call is named, with what it gives:

  ```
  `insert-keeps-sorted`: not verified
    at harness-insert.fx:20:47, `(assert (sorted? (insert e xs)))`: `assert`'s
    requires: not proved, as the proof knows too little of a call. It holds
    for e = 0, xs = (the ints nil); but for all the proof knows, (sorted?
    (insert 0 (the ints nil))) could be #f, where it is #t. An `ensures`
    saying what that returns may let it be proved.
  ```
- **beyond the prover**: the solver decides neither way.

A specification the verifier leaves open and `fixpt test` finds a
counterexample to is false; one it finds none to may be true but beyond
the prover (`tests/programs/specs/square.fx`), or false of an input not
drawn.

## Queries: `,query` and `fixpt query` (2026-10-27)

Questions about a program's definitions by their types and effects, at
the REPL (`,query KIND ARG`, of what was defined there: each form typed,
each file loaded) and from the command line (`fixpt query FILE… --
QUERY`, the files checked in order as `fixpt run` checks them). The
sources are checked again, keeping each expression's facts, and each
answer is a definition, or an item of a module defined or loaded, with
its type and place (`file:line:col`). `fixpt_fx26::query`. Any query
may end `around CONTEXT`, for the local names in scope there too.

| Query | Answers |
|---|---|
| `effect E` | definitions whose own effect has every atom of `E`, as `(write @ed)`; then, apart, those that may once instantiated: their effect has an effect variable, or the same atom at a region variable |
| `uses T` | definitions whose type mentions `T`, written or inferred |
| `returns T` | procedures that give a `T` |
| `constructs T` | those that give one without needing one: base constructors |
| `transforms T` | those that give one only for one: inductive constructors, transformers |
| `consumes T` | procedures that take a `T` and give none back for it |
| `mutates T` | procedures that need a `T` and may write a region where something `T` holds can be written (a ref, an array, a cell, a pair, a bloblet not frozen): in their own effect, or that of a procedure they take or return |

`T` is a type as written in the program's scope at its end, or a
generative type's bare name, which matches it applied to anything. Types
named by `define-type` are transparent, so a type of the same structure
under another name is the same type.

**Giving and needing.** As the customer given a value sees it: a value
gives a `T` it is or holds, or that a procedure in it returns, or calls
the customer's callback with; it needs a `T` that a procedure in it
takes, or that a callback it takes returns. A `T` it gives is *tolled*
when reaching it means calling a procedure whose arguments oblige the
customer to make a `T`: one is in a parameter as data, or as what a
callback the customer writes returns, but not as what such a callback is
given, which the customer receives. Dually, a `T` the customer gives is a *trade* when
the exchange gives one back: the procedure the customer calls with it
returns one, or the callback that must return it was given one.
`constructs` lists those that give a `T` untolled; `transforms`, those
that give one tolled; `consumes`, those that take one in no trade. One
procedure may be several. So, writing `->` for a procedure:

| Type | Is |
|---|---|
| `() -> T` | constructs |
| `T -> unit` | consumes |
| `T -> T` | transforms |
| `(() -> T) -> unit` | consumes: the customer makes each |
| `((() -> T) -> unit) -> unit` | constructs: the customer's callback is given a maker |
| `((T -> T) -> unit) -> unit` | transforms: it is given a transformer |
| `((() -> T) (T -> unit) -> unit) -> unit` | constructs and consumes: a maker that needs nothing, and a taker that gives nothing back |

This is what the types allow: a procedure that may call back with a `T`
is listed though it never does, and one whose `T` the type does not
show (made inside, kept in a global) is not.

**Local names, instantiated.** After the definitions, and those that
may, come the local names the program binds to a polymorphic definition
instantiated so that it answers where the definition, as it is, does
not: `(let ((wipe (proj clear! @ed))) …)` for `effect (write @ed)`, with
the definition it is in, and what it instantiates. Unnamed uses, such as
a call of `car` at a list of `T`s, are left out: there are hundreds in a
program of any size, and they say little. A global bound so is a
definition, of its type there.

**Around a context.** `QUERY around CONTEXT` also lists the local names
that answer in scope there, each with its type, place and definition: a
lambda's parameters, a `let`'s and a `letrec`'s names. The context is a
place, `FILE:LINE:COL` (the names whose scope holds it), a definition or a
module's item by name (`with-text`, `rope.fx:rope-insert`: every local
bound in it), or a file, a source or one loaded (every local bound in
it). `around`, not `at`: the place is where to look, not where a `T` is.

**Not yet**: an entry of types the REPL held for types not defined yet
(`,pending`) is not among the sources; a `tagcase` arm's names and a
`typecase`'s are not among the locals.

2. **Done (2026-09-25): a checking REPL**, `fixpt --dialect fx26 repl`, with
   `eval` and `run` to match. At first it checks
   and does not run. It prints each form's type and effect, with the
   speculative checking the FX-87 and FX-91 REPLs already have
   (`crates/fixpt-cli/src/speculate.rs`). It is cheap, and it makes every
   later step something you can try interactively, not only through
   tests.
3. **Done (2026-09-25): typed prompts, composable continuations and
   marks**, as "Control, typed" now states them. Each rule has a test that a
   wrong rule would fail (`crates/fixpt-fx26/tests/control.rs`).
4. **Done (2026-09-25): bidirectional checking** (`crates/fixpt-fx26/src/infer.rs`,
   `tests/bidirectional.rs`). A definition is checked against its
   signature. A `lambda` checked against a subroutine type needs no
   parameter types, and against a `poly` type needs no `plambda`. A
   polymorphic operator is projected implicitly, by matching its parameters
   against the arguments and its result against the expected type (local
   type inference). A type binder must be solved; an effect binder nothing
   constrains is `pure`; a region binder nothing constrains is a fresh
   region, which is sound and is what lets `(car (cons 1 #t))` check with
   no annotation. `(the T E)` asks for check mode. Every step-3 control case
   also checks with no `proj` at all.
   **Bounds from both sides** (2026-10-08, `TODO.md` §66, the user's): a
   type binder is not solved by the first thing said of it. The arguments
   bound it from below (their types, joined), the expected result from
   above (met), and a position inside something mutable (a pair's
   contents, an array's, a reference's), being invariant, fixes it. A binder
   whose bounds have met, or that is fixed, is known, and tells the
   arguments still to be checked what they are, as the expected type always
   did (so every pair of `(cons a (cons b nil))` is in the expected region).
   One whose bounds have not met is not known yet, whichever bound it has:
   an argument whose parameter mentions it bounds it too, rather than being
   told (a variable says its type; a call, checked against the upper bounds
   where there are some, else what is known so far, its own result there,
   so that it is still told its regions). So `(eq? a b)`, `a` narrowed and
   `b` not, needs no `proj`: each bounds `eq?`'s type from below, and the
   larger is it. Until the end the solution
   is the fixed type, else the lower bound, else the upper; the result is
   then subsumed into what is expected. So `(array-ref a i)`, `a` an
   `(arrayof int @r)`, checks where a union with `int` in it is expected
   (`programs/bidirectional/bounds.fx`), and where a `string` is, no type
   is both, and it is refused (`unions/bounds-apart.fx`). Both checkers
   (`infer.rs`, `Bounds`; `check-bounds.fx`).
5. **Done (2026-09-25): lowering to annotated Scheme, and running**
   (`crates/fixpt-fx26/src/lower.rs`, `session.rs`, `runtime.scm`;
   `tests/run.rs`). The output is legal Scheme that keeps what the checker
   proved. It is not a plain erasure. This follows the decision of
   2026-09-21: "stop erasing to Scheme source, you're throwing away compiler
   info" was resolved Twobit's way, not by bypassing Scheme. What the checker
   proved travels as inert quoted `%fx-note` data, which the expander reads
   into the Core IR's facts table with a basis of `Checked`. Three claims so
   far:
   - `integrable`, on an application of a standard binding that is a Scheme
     primitive of the same meaning. The initial environment cannot be
     assigned, and a later `define` of the name *shadows* it: it is lowered to
     a new global, `fx:name:2`.
   - `pure`, on an application whose masked effect is `pure`.
   - `no-escape`, on an expression that allocates when masking removed every
     allocation, and whose value is first-order data: a closure could hold
     an allocation its type does not name
     (`docs/research/soundness-findings.md`, F6).

   The compiled engine turns an integrable `+` into one `prim` instruction,
   and `tests/run.rs` checks the disassembly. `plambda`, `proj` and `the`
   lower to their bodies. A program's names are prefixed `fx:`, so they can
   neither capture nor clobber a Scheme name. Every example runs on both
   engines with the same answer.

   Still to carry: region facts beyond `no-escape`, and control facts such as
   "this prompt's body never aborts past it". Direct lowering to Core IR,
   skipping the text, stays possible, but only if a claim turns out to be
   something Scheme text cannot carry. So far none has.
6. **Done (2026-09-25): the eager reader in FX-26**
   (`crates/fixpt-fx26/src/eager-reader.fx`, 521 lines against the Scheme
   version's 444). It is a port procedure for procedure, and it keeps the
   Scheme reader's procedure names and meanings, so the same driver runs it:
   `fixpt_scheme::eager::EagerReader::attach(session, "fx:")`. It is now the
   third reader. `crates/fixpt-fx26/tests/eager.rs` checks it on the same
   inputs, on both engines:
   - the same data as the Rust reader;
   - the same status as the Scheme reader on every prefix of a tricky
     input;
   - the same error at the same position;
   - the same hole closers;
   - the same parse stack, read out of a suspended continuation's marks with
     `marks-of`.
   Both readers' sources read to the same data.

   What the port needed that the language did not have:
   - **Programs whose definitions refer to each other in any order.** A
     whole program is taken in two passes. `define-type`, `define-effect`
     and every signature come first, then the definitions in order
     (`Checker::declare_ahead`).
   - **`define-effect`**, since nearly every reading procedure has the one
     effect `reads`.
   - **Character literals, and `cond`, `and`, `or` and `let*`** as derived
     forms. `cond` needs an `else`, since FX has no unspecified value.
   - **Character, string and comparison operations.** Strings are immutable,
     so they are pure.
   - **An opaque, immutable `datum` type** for the Scheme data a reader
     produces, built and inspected by `datum-` operations (a union since
     2026-10-08, taken apart as Scheme's data are: "Datums", above). The
     FX-26 reader's
     data *are* Scheme data, so it can be compared with the others directly.
   - **Two inference refinements.** The expected result type is used
     before the arguments, so `(cons a (cons b nil))` puts every pair in the
     expected region. And `void` solves an otherwise unconstrained binder,
     as the result of a loop that never returns needs.

   Writing it found two things. First, the checker was right both times it
   rejected a signature: `list-entry` and `eager-state-data` allocate in the
   reader's region, which the arguments make visible, so the allocation
   cannot be masked. Second, a latent bug in `EagerReader`: it held the feed
   procedure across calls that may collect. The FX-26 reader allocates
   enough to move it, and that corrupted the heap.
7. **Done (2026-09-25): the speculation driver checks the licence**
   before running anything (`crates/fixpt-fx26/src/licence.rs`,
   `tests/licence.rs`). An effect is licensed for a driver that owns some
   regions when every atom of it is either an allocation, anywhere, or a
   read, write or control effect on an owned region. An effect variable
   never is. Two drivers check it:
   - **The FX-26 REPL** runs a finished expression as it is typed, with a
     small step budget, and shows its value before `Enter`, but only if its
     effect is licensed for a driver that owns *none* of the program's
     regions. What masking already removed, such as private allocation and
     a `cwcc` whose region nothing else can name, is not an obstacle. A
     write to a shared cell is not run, and the hint says why: `not run
     early: it may (write @c)`.
   - **The Scheme REPL's reader.** `fixpt --reader fx26 repl` reads input
     with the eager reader written in FX-26, which runs on every keystroke.
     Before any of it runs, every entry point's latent effect must be
     licensed: allocation, or effects on the regions the reader is given.
     A reader that reached outside them would be refused, naming what it
     would have done, and the REPL falls back to the Scheme reader; a test
     doctors one to check this. Loaded into the REPL's own session, it
     behaves as the Scheme reader does.

   **A licence is masking relative to an observer** (settled 2026-09-25).
   Masking asks what anything outside an expression can see. A licence asks
   what the user's program can see of code run early, repeatedly, or not at
   all. So which regions count as the driver's must be *established*, as
   masking establishes privacy, not asserted. The first version asserted
   them: a list of the reader's region names, which are global constants any
   program could write. Then the reader declared `(private-regions @s @e @m
   @c)`, each name standing, in that program, for a fresh uninterned region.
   Now (2026-10-08, the user's) the reader and the parser are module files
   of their regions (`(module-parameters ((rs region) …))`), each loading the
   one before at its own, and the front end makes the last at the regions
   it names (`reader.fx`): a `plambda` over them. That the reader touches
   only what it is given is its type: whichever regions the binders become,
   each entry point's latent effect is on those and on no region of its
   own choosing, since code under the binders can name no other
   (`Checker::parametric_licence`); and the entry points as the driver calls
   them touch only the regions the reader was made at. `private-regions` is
   gone from the language. A test shows that a module file naming a region
   of its own (`@user`) is refused. The two things a licence needs that
   masking does not are a step budget, since running early may not
   terminate, and showing failures rather than raising them. Neither
   termination nor failure is an effect.

## Open questions

- ~~**Crate structure.**~~ **A fresh crate, `fixpt-fx26`, not depending on
  `fixpt-fx87`.** FX-87 and FX-91 share no FX-specific code, and that is
  deliberate. Each port follows its own reference file for file: FX-87's
  Common Lisp (`mit-psrg-fx/fx87/old-impl`) and FX-91's Scheme
  (`extracted/fx91`), which were separate codebases. That is what makes a
  divergence traceable. So they should not be merged, and FX-26 should not be
  bolted onto either of them. FX-26 borrows FX-87's algorithms by reading
  them: regions, masking and subtyping, the parts the control-effects paper
  builds on. What really is common to all three is shared infrastructure,
  and most of it already is (reader profiles, heap, engines, the Scheme
  session, the conformance harness). The one remaining duplicate looked like
  the "check, erase to Scheme, run" session, about 160 lines with the same
  shape in each dialect. When FX-26 became its third user (step 5), what the
  three really shared turned out to be one call: evaluate lowered text and
  keep the program's output apart from its value. That already exists, as
  `fixpt_scheme::Session::eval_capturing`, so FX-26 uses it and no new crate
  was needed. The rest is dialect-specific: what to check, how to lower, and
  how to print.
- ~~**Answer types.** Does the region discipline avoid answer-type
  modification, or only postpone it?~~ **Neither — they are separate
  problems.** Regions and control effects say *where* control can go: `goto`
  and `comefrom` on `r`, masked at the prompt. As a bonus, an unmasked control
  effect at top level means "may abort to a prompt that is not there", which
  is currently only a run-time error. Answer types say *what type* crosses the
  boundary, and regions say nothing about that. The problem is avoided instead
  by **fixing the answer type in the tag**: `(prompt-tag A H D R)`, where
  every prompt for the tag has a body producing `A` and a handler of type
  `H → A`. A captured continuation is then `σ → A`, with `A` read off the tag
  rather than found by a dynamic search. (`D`, the effect bound, came later;
  see "Control, typed".) This is Typed Racket's approach, and it
  rules out `shift`/`reset` tricks that change the answer type (Danvy &
  Filinski 1989; Asai & Kameyama 2007), such as `(reset (… (shift k k)))`.
  Every use the tooling makes fits a fixed answer type: the eager reader
  (`k : char → state`), the resumable hole, and `%toplevel-run`.
- ~~**Surface.**~~ **The lexical differences are small; the type languages
  are what differ, and FX-26 takes FX-87's.** The reader profiles already
  cover the lexical side: case folding, `#t` as a symbol or a boolean,
  brackets as symbol characters or projection sugar, `#u` or `#U`. A reader
  written in FX-26 would handle it just as easily. The substantive difference
  is that FX-87's effects are indexed by regions and FX-91's standard module
  has none. Compare `car : (poly ((r region)) (poly ((t1 type) (t2 type))
  (subr (read r) ((pairof t1 t2 r)) t1)))` with `car : (poly ((t type))
  (subr read ((l (listof t))) t))`. FX-26 needs regions: both the licence
  ("no read or write on a shared region") and the control effects are
  about regions. From FX-91 it takes inference machinery for the local half
  of bidirectional checking, and modules only if something needs them.

  **FX-26 has a lexical profile of its own** (settled 2026-09-25). It took
  FX-87's at first, by default rather than by decision, and case folding
  surprised us once. `SyntaxProfile::FX26` is Scheme's, with two changes:
  - `#u` on its own is the unit value, beside R7RS's `#u8(…)` bytevectors,
    since the `8` keeps them apart;
  - `[` and `]` are **reserved**: reading one is an error. FX-91 used them
    as projection sugar, and FX-26 has not decided what they are for, so
    keeping them unused means a later meaning breaks no program.

  The eager reader written in FX-26 reads this profile when started with
  `eager-start-fx26`, and reads FX-26 programs, its own source among them,
  to exactly what the Rust reader does. The dialect is a continuation mark
  set around the whole parse. A checkpoint carries it, so no procedure's
  signature had to change. The FX-26 REPL now reads its input with that
  reader, once its licence is checked: FX-26 code reading FX-26 code. It is
  loaded under a global prefix of its own (`fx26-reader:`), so a user's
  definitions cannot replace its procedures. That is isolation at the level
  of names, where the reader's being a `plambda` over its regions gives it
  at the level of regions.
- ~~**A declared kernel.**~~ **Declared: see "The kernel" above.** FX-26
  has an explicitly declared minimal kernel, a subset of the full language,
  with everything else defined by translation into it. There is direct
  precedent: the control-effects paper states its rules over *KFX*, "the
  kernel language of FX-87". Step 1 fixed the grammar, and it was reviewed
  on 2026-09-25.
- **Syntax.** FX had no macros. Does FX-26 need them before the expander is
  ported to it, or does the port create the need?

[^cellular]: "Cellular" would be called "threaded" in the Forth community: code as
a sequence of cells (references to routines, and their operands), run by an inner
interpreter. This repository says "cellular" throughout (the user's decision,
2026-09-27).
