# Non-regular type families and subtyping: a survey

Research note, 2026-09-27. No network was used.

- **Memory (firm)**: from memory, and I am confident of authors, venue, year

## Summary

- **Equality** of first-order non-regular types (nested datatypes, families
  whose arguments grow) is decidable. It is the same problem as DPDA
  equivalence. The known algorithms are deep and far too costly to implement
  in a type checker.
- **Subtyping** of such types is undecidable once there is any real ordering:
  width subtyping on sums, or even one pair `a < b` of leaf types. It is
  language inclusion, not equivalence, and inclusion is undecidable already
  for the simplest deterministic grammars.
- **The useful direction is the hard one.** "Not a subtype" is
  semi-decidable: a finite path where the two types disagree proves it.
  "Is a subtype" is not semi-decidable. So no procedure can find every true
  `≤`. Any procedure that answers "yes" will sometimes answer "unknown" to a
  true question.
- **What stays decidable and cheap** is what GHC and OCaml do: a
  non-regular family is compared with itself only, by congruence on its
  declared variance, never unfolded against a structure. That is nominal
  treatment. It is polynomial.
- **GADT variance** (Scherer and Rémy): a GADT index can be covariant only if
  every type it is equated with is *upward-closed*, which in FX-26, with
  width sums and effect/region subsumption, few types are. Default indices to
  invariant, or use their "subtyping constraint" form.
- **Recommendation** (last section): nominal congruence for non-regular
  families; unfolding, generalization and cyclic proof only as a later,
  optional layer that answers yes, no, or "unknown" with a witness.

## 1. Equality of non-regular recursive types

### The reduction to DPDA equivalence

- **Regular types** are regular trees: finitely many distinct subtrees. That
  is what FX-26 has today. Equality and Amadio–Cardelli subtyping are
  polynomial on them (Kozen, Palsberg and Schwartzbach, "Efficient recursive
  subtyping", MSCS 1995; **Memory (firm)**).
- **A family whose arguments change** as it recurses, like
  `(nest a) = a × (nest (a × a))`, denotes a tree with infinitely many
  distinct subtrees. Such trees are the *algebraic* trees: those generated by
  a first-order recursion scheme, a grammar of first-order term rewriting
  rules (Courcelle, "Fundamental properties of infinite trees", TCS 25, 1983;
  **Memory (firm)**).
- **Solomon** ("Type definitions with parameters", POPL 1978;
  **Memory (firm)** for the paper, **Memory (check)** for the exact direction
  of the reductions) studied exactly FX-26's question: type definitions with
  parameters, compared structurally. He showed equivalence of such
  definitions reduces to equivalence of deterministic pushdown automata
  (DPDA), then a famous open problem. My recollection is that he showed the
  problems inter-reducible, so a type-checker solution would have solved DPDA
  equivalence.
- **Courcelle** related equivalence of recursive program schemes and of
  algebraic trees to DPDA equivalence in the same way ("A representation of
  trees by languages", TCS 1978; **Memory (check)**).

### Decidability

- **Sénizergues** proved DPDA equivalence decidable ("L(A)=L(B)?
  decidability results from complete formal systems", TCS 251, 2001;
  announced ICALP 1997; Gödel Prize 2002; **Memory (firm)**). The shape of
  the proof matters for us: two semi-decision procedures run side by side.
  One enumerates proofs in a complete deduction system for equations between
  configurations; the other enumerates candidate distinguishing words. A
  simplified proof is TCS 281, 2002 (**Memory (check)**). He extended it to
  bisimulation of equational graphs of finite out-degree (FOCS 1998;
  **Memory (check)**).
- **Stirling** gave a proof by tableaux ("Decidability of DPDA equivalence",
  TCS 255, 2001; **Memory (firm)**) and then a primitive recursive upper
  bound ("Deciding DPDA equivalence is primitive recursive", ICALP 2002;
  **Memory (firm)**).
- **Jančar** recast the problem as bisimulation equivalence of *first-order
  grammars*: labelled transition systems whose states are first-order terms
  and whose rules are root rewrites ("Decidability of DPDA language
  equivalence via first-order grammars", LICS 2012; "Bisimulation equivalence
  of first-order grammars", ICALP 2014; **Memory (firm)**). This is the
  formulation closest to FX-26: a state is a type term, an action is "take
  field i" or "take tag k", and a rule is "unfold family F". His proofs use
  a "balancing" step that replaces a deep subterm by an equivalent shallow
  one. That is the theory's analogue of our generalization step.

### Complexity

- **First-order grammars in general** (nondeterministic, bisimilarity):
  ACKERMANN-complete (Jančar and Schmitz, "Bisimulation equivalence of
  first-order grammars is ACKERMANN-complete", LICS 2019; **Memory (firm)**).
- **Pushdown bisimilarity** has a non-elementary lower bound (Benedikt,
  Göller, Kiefer and Murawski, "Bisimilarity of pushdown automata is
  nonelementary", LICS 2013; **Memory (firm)**).
- **DPDA equivalence** (our deterministic case): upper bound primitive
  recursive (Stirling), which I recall was later tightened to TOWER, with
  Jančar's methods (**Memory (check)**). The lower bound is only P-hardness.
  The gap is enormous and open.
- **Simple grammars** (one-state, real-time DPDAs: every family unfolding
  immediately shows a constructor) are much easier. Equivalence is decidable
  (Korenjak and Hopcroft, FOCS/SWAT 1966; **Memory (firm)**), and bisimilarity
  of normed context-free processes is polynomial (Hirshfeld, Jerrum and
  Moller, "A polynomial algorithm for deciding bisimilarity of normed
  context-free processes", TCS 158, 1996; **Memory (firm)**). Context-free
  processes are bisimulation-decidable in general (Christensen, Hüttel and
  Stirling, Information and Computation 1995; **Memory (firm)**).

### Practical algorithms and implementations

- **DPDA equivalence**: I know of one implementation, LALBLC by Henry and
  Sénizergues (CIAA 2013; **Memory (check)**). It is a research tool.
- **Context-free session types** are the best practical precedent. Session
  types with sequential composition are non-regular, and their equivalence is
  bisimilarity of simple grammars (Thiemann and Vasconcelos, "Context-free
  session types", ICFP 2016; **Memory (firm)**). Almeida, Mordido and
  Vasconcelos give an algorithm that is fast in practice, built on the
  Jančar–Moller style of expansion tree with congruence-based pruning, and it
  runs in the FreeST compiler ("Deciding the bisimilarity of context-free
  session types", TACAS 2020; **Memory (firm)**; the details of the pruning
  rules are **Memory (check)**).
- **The lesson**: practical equivalence checkers for non-regular types exist
  when the grammar is *simple* (every unfolding is guarded by a constructor,
  deterministic), and they run a budget-free, pruned search, not the general
  DPDA algorithm.

### Higher-order families, and Gauthier–Pottier

- **Higher-order families** (families taking families) generate trees by
  higher-order recursion schemes. As far as I know, equality of such trees is
  open in general (**Memory (check)**). Ong's decidability result for them
  (LICS 2006) is MSO model checking, not equivalence.
- **Gauthier and Pottier**, "Numbering matters: first-order canonical forms
  for second-order recursive types" (ICFP 2004; **Memory (firm)**). Here
  "second-order" means *quantifiers* (`∀`) inside recursive types, not type
  operators. They number binders de Bruijn style so that a recursive type
  with `∀` becomes a *regular first-order* tree; equality and unification
  then reduce to the first-order case, in near-linear time.
  - **It does not apply to non-regular families.** A nested datatype is not
    regular after any renumbering.
  - **It does apply to FX-26's `poly` cycles**, the divergence recorded in
    `recursive-subtyping.md`: a `poly` on a cycle is exactly their case.
    Worth reading if canonical forms for `poly` come back.
- **Equi-recursive System Fω.** Cai, Giarrusso and Ostermann ("System F-omega
  with equirecursive types for datatype-generic programming", POPL 2016;
  **Memory (check)**) take equi-recursive type operators seriously. My memory
  is that they restrict the recursion to keep type equivalence decidable, and
  note the general problem's link to the hard cases above; check before
  relying on it.

## 2. Subtyping of non-regular types

### Subtyping is inclusion, and inclusion is undecidable

Think of a type tree as a deterministic transition system: a state is a type,
an action is "field i", "tag k", "argument" or "result", and each state shows
a label (its head constructor). For deterministic systems, simulation is the
same as trace inclusion. So:

- **Equality** of type trees is *equivalence* of deterministic systems:
  decidable (section 1).
- **Width subtyping** (`[A|B] ≤ [A|B|C]`) makes `≤` *inclusion* of path sets,
  with label order at the leaves and direction flipped under contravariance.

What is known about inclusion and simulation:

- **DPDA language inclusion is undecidable**, a classical result from the
  1960s (**Memory (firm)** for the fact; I do not recall the first paper).
- **Inclusion is undecidable even for simple deterministic languages**, the
  real-time one-state case where equivalence is polynomial (Friedman, "The
  inclusion problem for simple languages", TCS 1, 1976; **Memory (firm)**).
  This is the key fact. The simplest non-regular grammars already make
  inclusion undecidable.
- **Simulation on BPA** (context-free processes) is undecidable; in fact
  every equivalence in van Glabbeek's spectrum except bisimilarity is
  (Groote and Hüttel, "Undecidable equivalences for basic process algebra",
  Information and Computation 115, 1994; **Memory (firm)**).
- **Simulation between a pushdown system and a finite-state system** is
  decidable, EXPTIME-complete (Kučera and Mayr, "Simulation preorder over
  simple process algebras", Information and Computation 2002, and related
  papers; **Memory (check)** for venue and exact bound). In type terms: a
  non-regular type against a *regular* one is decidable. That is a real
  fragment: `(nest int) ≤ R` for a regular `R`.
- **Simulation on one-counter nets** is decidable, on one-counter automata
  not (Abdulla and Čerāns 1998; Jančar, Moller and Sawa 1999;
  **Memory (check)**). Size-indexed lists without index arithmetic are
  roughly one-counter.
- **Subtyping of context-free session types** is undecidable, and FreeST
  implements a sound, incomplete check (Silva, Mordido and Vasconcelos,
  "Subtyping context-free session types", CONCUR 2023; **Memory (firm)** for
  the result, **Memory (check)** that the proof reduces from Friedman's simple
  language inclusion). This is the closest published precedent to FX-26's
  question: a non-regular structural type language, with subtyping, in a real
  compiler.

### Does adding subtyping make FX-26 undecidable?

Yes, if non-regular families are compared by unfolding. A sketch of why
(my own argument, not from a paper):

- **With width subtyping on sums**, encode a simple grammar as a family: each
  nonterminal is a family whose unfolding is a sum with one tag per terminal.
  The paths of `(F)` are the words of its language. Then `F ≤ G` is language
  inclusion, which is Friedman's undecidable problem.
- **Removing width subtyping is not enough.** Keep only one ordered pair of
  leaf types, `a < b` (a numeric tower, a frozen/mutable pair, a smaller
  effect, a region that outlives another: FX-26 has several). Take two types
  with the same shape, a full binary tree, whose leaves are labelled `a` or
  `b` by two simple grammars. Then `t ≤ u` iff every path labelled `b` in `t`
  is labelled `b` in `u`: inclusion again.
- **Invariant parameters are not enough either.** The ordering that breaks
  decidability is in the body, at the leaves, not in the arguments. An
  invariant index stops the index from moving, not the body from being
  compared by inclusion.

### The useful direction

- **"Not ≤" is semi-decidable.** A failure of `t ≤ u` on trees always shows
  at a finite path: a head mismatch, a missing tag, a bad leaf pair. Unfold
  both sides breadth-first and you will find it if it exists.
- **"≤" is therefore not semi-decidable** when it is undecidable (its
  complement is semi-decidable; if both were, it would be decidable).
- **Consequence.** A type checker needs to *prove* `≤`, which is the
  direction that cannot be complete. Every "yes" procedure is sound and
  incomplete. The best we can offer is: yes (with a proof), no (with a
  witness), or unknown. This is the same shape as Sénizergues' proof, minus
  the completeness of the "yes" side.

### Restrictions that stay decidable

| Restriction                                                        | Decidable?        | Cost                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------- | ---------------------- |
| Regular families only (FX-26 today)                                | yes               | polynomial             |
| Non-regular families nominal: congruence on declared variance only | yes               | polynomial             |
| Non-regular vs regular only (one side regular)                     | yes (Kučera–Mayr) | exponential            |
| No ordering anywhere in non-regular bodies (`≤` collapses to `=`)  | yes (Sénizergues) | impractical, TOWER-ish |
| Invariant indices, unfolding allowed                               | no                | —                      |
| No width subtyping, but some ordered leaf pair                     | no                | —                      |
| Unfolding plus budget plus generalization                          | sound, incomplete | bounded by the budget  |

Notes on the rows:

- **Nominal congruence.** `(F σ) ≤ (F σ')` iff `σᵢ ≺ᵥᵢ σ'ᵢ` by `F`'s declared
  variance; `(F σ)` against anything that is not an `F` fails (or, if `F` is
  allowed to be unfolded against structure, only through its *one* regular
  unfolding step, not recursively). The arguments are finite type graphs, so
  Amadio–Cardelli on them terminates. This is sound only if `F`'s declared
  variance is checked against its body (section 4 for GADTs), and it is
  incomplete against the structural reading: a phantom or unused parameter
  makes `(F a) ≤ (F b)` true structurally while congruence demands `a ≤ b`.
- **Kennedy and Pierce's analogue.** For nominal subtyping with variance and
  generic inheritance, they show undecidability when three things combine:
  contravariance, multiple instantiation inheritance, and *expansive*
  inheritance (a class whose supertype mentions it at a growing argument).
  Removing any one gives decidability ("On decidability of nominal subtyping
  with variance", FOOL/WOOD 2007; **Memory (firm)** for the result,
  **Memory (check)** for the venue). Their "expansive" is FX-26's
  "arguments change as it recurses", and their proof of the non-expansive case
  is FX-26's current argument: finitely many pairs reach the trail.
- **Java.** Grigore showed Java's generic subtyping Turing-complete ("Java
  generics are Turing complete", POPL 2017; **Memory (firm)**). Tate, Leung
  and Lerner ("Taming wildcards in Java's type system", PLDI 2011;
  **Memory (firm)**) and Greenman, Muehlboeck and Tate ("Getting F-bounded
  polymorphism into shape", PLDI 2014; **Memory (firm)**) find restrictions
  that real code already obeys. The second's method, surveying code for which
  restriction costs nothing, is the right way to choose ours.

## 3. Sound but incomplete procedures used in practice

### Bisimulation up to congruence, and other up-to techniques

- **Bonchi and Pous**, "Checking NFA equivalence with bisimulations up to
  congruence" (POPL 2013; **Memory (firm)**). Hopcroft–Karp builds a
  bisimulation pair by pair. They skip a pair when it already follows from the
  pairs so far by *congruence closure*, which can cut the search
  exponentially. The same algorithm returns a counterexample word on failure.
- **Theory of up-to techniques**: Sangiorgi, "On the bisimulation proof
  method" (MSCS 1998; **Memory (firm)**); Pous and Sangiorgi, "Enhancements
  of the bisimulation proof method" (in *Advanced Topics in Bisimulation and
  Coinduction*, CUP 2012; **Memory (firm)**); Pous, "Coinduction all the way
  up" (LICS 2016; **Memory (firm)**). An up-to function is safe when it is
  *compatible*; compatible functions compose. For a precongruence like
  FX-26's `≤`, "up to context" (from `X ≤ Y` infer `C[X] ≤ C[Y]` for a
  covariant `C`) and "up to transitivity" are the relevant ones.
  (**Memory (check)**: that both are compatible in the strong, deterministic
  setting; I believe so.)
- **Bisimulation bases** for context-free processes are the older form of the
  same idea: a finite relation whose congruence closure is a bisimulation
  (Caucal, "Graphes canoniques de graphes algébriques", RAIRO 1990;
  Christensen, Hüttel and Stirling 1995; **Memory (check)** for Caucal's
  exact title). Hüttel and Stirling's tableau for BPA ("Actions speak louder
  than words", LICS 1991; **Memory (firm)**) has a SUB rule that rewrites a
  goal by an earlier one: a cyclic proof with substitution.
- **FX-26 already does the first step**: the trail is a (plain, not up-to)
  coinductive candidate. Up to congruence means: a pending `(F σ) ≤ (G τ)`
  is closed if it follows from trail entries by variance and transitivity.

### Cyclic proofs

- **Brotherston**, "Cyclic proofs for first-order logic with inductive
  definitions" (TABLEAUX 2005; **Memory (firm)**); Brotherston and Simpson,
  "Sequent calculi for induction and infinite descent" (LICS 2007, JLC 2011;
  **Memory (firm)**); Brotherston, Gorogiannis and Petersen, "A generic
  cyclic theorem prover" (APLAS 2012, the Cyclist tool; **Memory (firm)**).
- **How they find cycles.** Proof search is ordinary backward search. At
  each new goal, look among its ancestors for one of which it is an instance
  (up to substitution, and weakening of hypotheses). If one is found, add a
  back-link. At the end, check a global soundness condition on the whole
  graph: every infinite path must have a trace that progresses infinitely
  often. Cyclist checks it as inclusion of Büchi automata.
- **For FX-26 the global condition is simpler.** Subtyping is a greatest fixed
  point, so a back-link is sound when at least one constructor lies between
  ancestor and descendant (guardedness), which FX-26's `grounded` check
  already guarantees for unfoldings. No descent argument is needed.
- **Cycles often need a lemma.** Without cut, a goal may never be an instance
  of an ancestor, only of a *generalization* of one. Cyclic provers that
  succeed on hard examples add lemma speculation or generalization
  (**Memory (check)** for specific papers; the phenomenon is well known).
- **Circular coinduction** (Roşu and Lucanu, "Circular coinduction: a proof
  theoretical foundation", CALCO 2009; Goguen, Lin and Roşu, ASE 2000;
  **Memory (firm)**) is the same idea for behavioural equality, with the same
  need for generalization.

### Generalization by anti-unification

- **Plotkin**, "A note on inductive generalization", and **Reynolds**,
  "Transformational systems and the algebraic structure of atomic formulas",
  both *Machine Intelligence 5*, 1970 (**Memory (firm)**). Independently
  defined the least general generalization of two terms. It is unique up to
  renaming and computed by one simultaneous walk: equal heads recurse,
  different subterms become a variable, and the same pair of subterms gets the
  same variable.
- **Inductive theorem provers.** Boyer and Moore generalize a goal by
  replacing common subterms by variables (*A Computational Logic*, 1979;
  **Memory (firm)**). Walsh's divergence critic watches for a sequence of
  subgoals that grow by the same "difference" and proposes the generalized
  lemma that covers them all ("A divergence critic for inductive proof", JAIR
  4, 1996; **Memory (firm)**). Ireland and Bundy's proof critics do similar
  things with rippling ("Productive use of failure in inductive proof", JAR
  1996; **Memory (firm)**). Walsh is exactly FX-26's plan: detect a growing
  chain, generalize, try to prove.
- **Supercompilation** gives the termination argument the budget lacks.
  Sørensen and Glück ("An algorithm of generalization in positive
  supercompilation", ILPS 1995; **Memory (firm)**) unfold until the new term
  *homeomorphically embeds* an ancestor, then replace the ancestor by the
  most specific generalization of the two and continue. Leuschel ("On the
  power of homeomorphic embedding for online termination", SAS 1998;
  **Memory (firm)**) studies the "whistle". By Kruskal's tree theorem the
  embedding is a well-quasi-order on terms over a finite signature, so every
  infinite branch whistles. With generalization that replaces rather than
  adds, the whole search terminates without a numeric budget.
- **For FX-26's chains.** Anti-unify `(nest (a×a)) ≤ (nest (b×b))` with
  `(nest ((a×a)×(a×a))) ≤ (nest ((b×b)×(b×b)))` and you get
  `(nest X) ≤ (nest Y)`, which only holds under a hypothesis `X ≤ Y`. So a
  generalized lemma is a *Horn clause*: hypotheses between the new
  variables, then the goal. The hypotheses come from pairing the two sides'
  new variables. For one family against itself this lemma is just its
  variance; generalization earns its keep only between *different* families
  or a family and a structure. The lemma must be proved with its variables
  opaque (only `X ≤ X`, and the hypotheses), so that any instance follows.

### Nested datatypes and polymorphic recursion in GHC and OCaml

- **Nested datatypes**: Bird and Meertens, "Nested datatypes" (MPC 1998);
  Bird and Paterson, "de Bruijn notation as a nested datatype" (JFP 1999);
  Okasaki, *Purely Functional Data Structures* (1998) (**Memory (firm)**).
- **Both languages make them nominal.** A `data` or `type … = …` datatype is
  generative. Its applications are compared by name and argument, never
  unfolded. That is why they need none of section 1: equality of `Nest a`
  and `Nest b` is equality of `a` and `b`.
- **OCaml refuses non-regular *structural* types.** Recursive type
  abbreviations (objects, polymorphic variants, `-rectypes`) must be regular;
  a definition like `type 'a t = < m : 'a list t >` is rejected with an error
  of the form "In this definition, a type variable … / type 'a list t should
  be 'a t" (**Memory (check)** for the exact wording, **Memory (firm)** for
  the rule). This is precisely FX-26's current rule, and OCaml has kept it.
- **Polymorphic recursion needs an annotation.** Inference is undecidable
  (Henglein, "Type inference with polymorphic recursion", TOPLAS 1993;
  Kfoury, Tiuryn and Urzyczyn, TOPLAS 1993; **Memory (firm)**; Mycroft 1984
  introduced the rule). Haskell accepts it with a type signature; OCaml with
  an explicit polymorphic annotation `let rec f : 'a. 'a nest -> int = …`
  (since 3.12; **Memory (check)** for the version). Checking with the
  annotation is easy.
- **GADTs in GHC** are solved by OutsideIn(X) (Vytiniotis, Peyton Jones,
  Schrijvers and Sulzmann, JFP 2011; **Memory (firm)**), again over nominal
  types. Type families are reduced with a depth budget (section 5).
- **Roles** (Breitner, Eisenberg, Peyton Jones and Weirich, "Safe zero-cost
  coercions for Haskell", ICFP 2014; **Memory (firm)**) are GHC's variance
  for representational equality. A GADT index gets the *nominal* role, the
  analogue of invariant. That is GHC's answer to section 4, taken without
  argument.
- **Code generation.** Polymorphic recursion over a nested type needs a
  uniform representation; monomorphizing compilers (Rust, MLton) cannot
  expand it and stop at a recursion limit (**Memory (firm)** for Rust's
  "reached the recursion limit while instantiating"). FX-26 on a Scheme-like
  runtime should be fine, but lowering should be checked.

## 4. GADTs with subtyping: Scherer and Rémy

Scherer and Rémy, "GADTs meet subtyping", ESOP 2013, LNCS 7792, pp. 554–573;
long version INRIA RR-8114, October 2012 (**Sure (read)**: both local
copies; this summary follows the long version).

### The question

When may a GADT parameter be declared covariant? Their example:

```
type +α expr =
  | Val   : α → α expr
  | Int   : int → int expr
  | Thunk : ∀β. β expr * (β → α) → α expr
  | Prod  : ∀βγ. β expr * γ expr → (β * γ) expr
```

It is covariant in OCaml, but would not be in a system with a top type.

### The criterion

- **Formal basis.** They instantiate Simonet and Pottier's HMG(X) (TOPLAS
  2007), whose soundness proof needs, for each constructor
  `K of ∃β[D]. τ`, the condition (req-SP)
  `σ t ≤ σ' t ∧ D[σ, ρ] ⟹ ∃ρ'. D[σ', ρ'] ∧ τ[ρ] ≤ τ[ρ']`.
  Their contribution is a syntactic, decidable check equivalent to it.
- **Upward closure.** A type `T[β]` is upward-closed if every supertype of an
  instance is again an instance: `T[ρ] ≤ σ'` implies `σ' = T[ρ']` with
  `ρ ≤ ρ'`. Downward closure is the same with `≥`. A GADT equation
  `α = T[β]` on a covariant `α` is sound iff `T` is upward-closed (for a
  contravariant `α`, downward-closed). The `Prod` case works because every
  supertype of a product is a product; `Int` works because `int` has no
  other supertype.
- **Counterexamples** (both in the paper): an object type `<m:int>` has the
  supertype `< >`, and a private type `file_descr = private int` has the
  supertype `int`. Either lets a covariant GADT forge an equality witness
  `(<>, <m:int>) eq` and cast unsoundly.
- **The syntactic judgment** `Γ ⊢ τ : v ⇒ v'` (their Figure 3). A variable
  must be used at exactly its variance (`w = v`, not `w ≥ v`); a constructor
  must be *v-closed* at its head, with its arguments checked recursively.
- **Repeated variables are the subtle part.** They "zip" the variances of
  several occurrences. Two invariant occurrences zip; an irrelevant
  occurrence zips with anything; two covariant ones do not. So `β * γ` is
  upward-closed but `β * β` is not (with `⊥`, `⊥ * ⊥ ≤ int * bool`, which is
  no instance of `β * β`). `β ref * β ref` is upward-closed, since `ref` is
  invariant.
- **The final criterion** (their equation (7)) is:
  `∃Γ, (Γᵢ). Γ ⊢ τ : + ∧ Γ = &ᵢ Γᵢ ∧ ∀i. Γᵢ ⊢ Tᵢ : vᵢ ⇒ (=)`.
  They prove it sound and complete relative to req-SP (completeness only for
  target variance `=`). Algorithmically, compute for each variable the set of
  variances that work, zip, intersect; accept if non-empty. It is a
  per-constructor syntactic check, as cheap as ordinary variance checking.
- **Incompleteness they note.** req-SP itself is incomplete for uninhabited
  constructors, and variance per parameter cannot describe `eq` (they suggest
  "domain information": subtyping only between inhabited instances).

### Non-monotonicity

Closure properties are *negative* facts about subtyping ("nothing else is
above `T`"). Adding subtyping to the language later (a top type, private
types, a new coercion) can destroy them. In OCaml, therefore:
- no type is downward-closed (private types);
- no arrow type is upward-closed (its domain would need downward closure);
- no object type but `< >` is upward-closed;
- datatypes are upward-closed if their components are.

They propose letting users declare a type upward- or downward-closed, like a
`final` class, which then forbids later extensions of the order around it.

### The alternative: subtyping constraints

Replace each equation `α = T[β]` by `α ≥ T[β]` (for a covariant `α`):

```
type +α expr =
  | Int  of [α ≥ int]. int
  | Prod of ∃βγ[α ≥ β * γ]. β expr * γ expr
  | …
```

Covariance is then immediate (check `⇒ (+)` instead of `⇒ (=)`) and needs no
closure properties, so it survives any extension of the order. `eval :
α expr → α` still types, with explicit upcasts. The price: matching gives a
weaker fact (`int ≤ α`, not `int = α`), and declarations are wordier. Emir,
Kennedy, Russo and Yu did this for C# ("Variance and generalized constraints
for C# generics", ECOOP 2006; **Memory (firm)**, and cited by Scherer and
Rémy).

### What it implies for FX-26

- **FX-26 has much non-atomic subtyping**: width sums, frozen covariance,
  region outlives, effect subsumption. So few types are upward-closed. A sum
  is not (a sum with more tags is above it). A function type is not (its
  domain is not downward-closed, and its effect can grow). Only base types
  with no supertypes, and products and frozen data built from them, are.
- **Default GADT indices to invariant.** This is what GHC (nominal roles)
  and OCaml (by default) do. It needs no closure analysis and is always
  sound.
- **Allow covariance by their check** only where it pays, and fix FX-26's
  closure facts now, before more subtyping is added, as part of the type
  language's contract (non-monotonicity).
- **Or offer subtyping-constraint constructors** (`α ≥ T`) for the
  covariant case; they suit a checker that already has subsumption.
- **Their setting is nominal.** GADTs there are named, generative types
  compared by variance, never unfolded. Their result is exactly the
  soundness condition that the "nominal congruence" row in section 2 needs:
  a family's declared variance must pass their check before congruence may
  use it.
- **Structural GADTs raise extra questions** the paper does not address. The
  structural reading of `(exp int)` is a sum of *guarded existentials* (the
  constructors whose index can be `int`), and constructors like
  `Fst : exp (a × b) → exp a` make it non-regular. Comparing those needs
  existentials compared under binder environments (as `poly` now is) as well
  as everything in section 2.

## 5. Error reporting for failed or undecided comparisons

### Witnesses for "no"

- **A distinguishing path** is the natural witness: a sequence of steps
  (field, tag, argument, result, unfold F) from the two roots to a pair of
  nodes that fail locally, with the current variance. For tree types it
  always exists when `≤` fails (section 2). Hopcroft–Karp and Bonchi–Pous
  return a shortest counterexample word for automata the same way
  (**Memory (firm)**).
- **Compilers** print the path as a nested trace. OCaml prints unification
  traces: "Type A is not compatible with type B. The method m has type … but
  is expected to have type …" (**Memory (firm)** for the shape).
  TypeScript prints "Types of property 'x' are incompatible", one line per
  step (**Memory (firm)**).
- **For FX-26** the trail already holds the path: record, for each pending
  pair, which step led to it, and on failure print the chain from the root.
  Breadth-first search for the failure gives the shortest path, which reads
  best.

### Reporting "unknown"

Precedents, all budgets with an escape hatch (**Memory (firm)** for the
existence of each, **Memory (check)** for exact wording and defaults):

- **GHC**: "Reduction stack overflow; size = 201 … Use -freduction-depth=0 to
  disable this check (any upper bound you could choose might fail
  unpredictably with minor updates to GHC, so disabling the check is
  recommended if you're sure that type checking should terminate)". It names
  the budget, the goal, and the knob, and warns that the knob is fragile.
- **Rust**: E0275, "overflow evaluating the requirement `…`", with "consider
  increasing the recursion limit by adding a `#![recursion_limit = "…"]`
  attribute". The new trait solver treats overflow as *ambiguity*, not
  failure, internally.
- **TypeScript**: TS2589, "Type instantiation is excessively deep and possibly
  infinite", and TS2321, "Excessive stack depth comparing types". Worse,
  when a comparison meets deeply nested instantiations of the same generic
  (depth ≥ a small constant) it returns "Maybe" and *treats it as related*.
  That is unsound by design; FX-26 should not copy it.
- **javac** historically overflowed its stack on expansive wildcard types
  (Tate, Leung and Lerner 2011). The lesson is to make "unknown" an answer,
  not a crash.

### What a good "unknown" says

- the original question, and that it is **undecided, not false**;
- the budget or whistle that stopped it;
- the **growing chain**: two or three pending comparisons, and the
  generalized lemma it tried, with the step where that proof failed or ran
  out;
- if a counterexample search was also run, how deep it went without finding
  one ("no difference within depth 12");
- a fix the user can apply: an annotation, or a user-stated lemma (below).

**User-stated lemmas.** Checking a lemma is much easier than finding one. If
FX-26 let a program state `∀X Y. X ≤ Y ⟹ (F X) ≤ (G Y)`, the checker would
only need one cyclic proof with that lemma on the trail as a hypothesis. This
mirrors polymorphic recursion: inference is undecidable, checking an
annotation is easy.

### Literature

There is little on explaining *undecided* type comparisons. On explaining
type errors generally: Haack and Wells, "Type error slicing in implicitly
typed higher-order languages" (ESOP 2003, SCP 2004; **Memory (firm)**);
Zhang, Myers, Vytiniotis and Peyton Jones, "Diagnosing type errors with
class" (PLDI 2015; **Memory (firm)**). Neither covers recursive-type
witnesses directly.

## What this means for FX-26

**Recommendation: treat non-regular families nominally, and keep structural
unfolding for regular ones.**

1. **Keep the regular rule for structural families.** It is what OCaml does
   for its structural types, it is Kennedy–Pierce's "non-expansive", and it
   is why the trail terminates today.
2. **Add non-regular families as named, generative types.** An application
   `(F σ)` is a node that is not unfolded during comparison. `(F σ) ≤ (F σ')`
   by `F`'s declared variance; `(F σ)` against anything else fails. Values
   enter and leave through constructors and matching (iso-recursive at that
   one point). This is decidable, polynomial, and fits the existing trail,
   because the arguments are ordinary finite type graphs.
   - This needs a representation change: today families are expanded at
     parse time (`Checker::knots`, `parse.rs`), so a family application does
     not survive into the checker. It must become a node.
3. **Check declared variances against bodies.** For ordinary families, the
   usual variance check. For GADT constructors, Scherer and Rémy's criterion.
   Default indices to invariant; allow covariance only through their
   upward-closure check or through `α ≥ T` constraints. Write down which FX-26
   types are upward- and downward-closed, and treat that list as a
   commitment.
4. **Polymorphic recursion by annotation only.** Functions over nested types
   must carry their polymorphic type, as in Haskell and OCaml.
5. **Size-indexed lists do not belong in this mechanism.** An index like
   `n - 1` is not a type to unfold; for symbolic `n` there is nothing to
   unfold. Give indices their own sort and a decision procedure (DML: Xi and
   Pfenning, "Dependent types in practical programming", POPL 1999;
   **Memory (firm)**; sized types: Hughes, Pareto and Sabry, POPL 1996;
   Abel, "Polarized subtyping for sized types", MSCS 2008; **Memory
   (firm)**).
6. **Structural comparison of non-regular families, if ever, as a separate
   and optional layer.** Section 2 shows it cannot be complete: subtyping
   with any ordering is undecidable, and the "yes" side is not even
   semi-decidable. If it is built:
   - run the proof search (unfold; close by trail up to variance and
     transitivity; on a whistle, anti-unify into a Horn lemma and prove it
     cyclically with its variables opaque) *and* a breadth-first
     counterexample search;
   - use homeomorphic embedding as the whistle, as in supercompilation, not
     a bare step count;
   - answer yes, no with a path, or unknown with the chain and the lemma
     tried; never treat unknown as yes;
   - let users state lemmas, which the checker proves.

   The best existing guide is FreeST: its equivalence algorithm for
   context-free session types (Almeida, Mordido and Vasconcelos 2020) and
   its sound, incomplete subtyping (Silva, Mordido and Vasconcelos 2023).
   Their grammars are *simple* (every unfolding guarded, deterministic);
   FX-26's `grounded` check gives the guarded half, and determinism holds
   for tree types.
7. **Separately**: Gauthier and Pottier's numbering is the right tool for the
   `poly`-on-a-cycle divergence in `recursive-subtyping.md`, not for this.
